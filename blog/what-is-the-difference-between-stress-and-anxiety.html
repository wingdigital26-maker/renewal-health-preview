What Is the Difference Between Stress and Anxiety? A Gentle Guide

By Lynette Wing, Clinical Homeopath, Naturopath, Mind-Body Practitioner · October 28, 2026

Stress is usually a response to something happening now, like a deadline or a hard conversation, and it tends to ease once the pressure passes. Anxiety often lingers without a clear cause, or keeps going long after the pressure is gone. In the body they can feel almost identical, because both use the same alarm system.

What is stress?

Stress is your body's response to a demand. A looming project, a sick child, a move, or a tight budget all ask something of you, and your nervous system rises to meet them. Stress hormones sharpen your focus, raise your heart rate, and send energy to your muscles so you can act.

In small, short doses, this is a healthy design. It helps you finish the report, catch the falling cup, and show up for the people who need you. Stress becomes a problem when the demand never ends and the body never gets the signal that it can rest.

What is anxiety?

Anxiety is a sense of worry, dread, or unease that often runs ahead of the moment. It asks "What if?" more than "What now?" You might feel anxious about something that has not happened, may never happen, or that you cannot name at all. The American Psychological Association describes anxiety as persistent, excessive worry that does not go away even when the stressor is gone.

Here is a simple way I explain it to clients. Stress is the body responding to a fire in the room. Anxiety is the body still smelling smoke after the fire is out, or bracing for a fire that has not started.

Why do stress and anxiety feel the same in the body?

Because they use the same wiring. Both switch on the sympathetic nervous system, the part of you designed for fight or flight. That is why either one can bring:

  • A racing or pounding heart
  • Tight shoulders, jaw, or chest
  • Shallow breathing
  • Digestive upset, such as a knotted or aching stomach. I explain this in my post on whether stress can cause stomach pain.
  • Trouble falling or staying asleep
  • Feeling wired but tired

Your body does not always know the difference between a real deadline and an imagined disaster. It simply hears "danger" and prepares. Over time, a body that stays in this state can feel the effects in many systems, including the heart and blood vessels. My post on whether stress can cause high blood pressure looks at that connection and when to involve your doctor.

Can stress turn into anxiety?

It can. When stress goes on for a long time, the nervous system can learn to stay on alert even after the pressure lifts. I call this the sympathetic lock: the body forgets how to stand down. What began as a response to real pressure becomes a background hum of worry.

Some people live in this state for years and look perfectly fine on the outside. If you are productive, organized, and quietly exhausted from worry, you may relate to my post on high-functioning anxiety.

How can I calm both stress and anxiety?

Because they share the same alarm system, many of the same gentle practices help both.

  1. Breathe with a longer exhale. Slow out-breaths are one of the clearest signals of safety you can give your body.
  2. Name what is real right now. Ask, "Is this happening, or am I bracing for it?" That question helps separate stress from anxiety.
  3. Move the energy. A short walk or gentle stretching helps the body complete the stress response instead of storing it.
  4. Protect your sleep and meals. A body that is rested and fed has more capacity to stay steady.
  5. Find support. You do not have to sort this out alone. My emotional overwhelm and nervous system support is designed for exactly this kind of season.

When should I see a doctor?

Please talk with your physician or a licensed mental health professional if worry or stress is constant, interferes with work, sleep, or relationships, or comes with panic, chest pain, or shortness of breath. Chest pain or trouble breathing should always be checked by a doctor right away. The NIMH overview of anxiety disorders explains when anxiety may need professional care. If you ever have thoughts of harming yourself, call or text 988 in the US, or call 911.

Pause for Reflection

Place a hand on your belly and let it rise and fall with your breath.

Ask gently: Is what I feel today about something in front of me, or about something I am bracing for? What would help my body believe it is safe in this moment?

"Be careful for nothing; but in every thing by prayer and supplication with thanksgiving let your requests be made known unto God" (Philippians 4:6). That verse is not a command to stop feeling. It is an invitation to hand the weight to Someone who can hold it.

FAQ

Is anxiety worse than stress?

Not necessarily. Both can be heavy. Stress usually eases when the pressure passes, while anxiety may linger without a clear cause. Either deserves care and attention when it starts affecting daily life.

Can you have stress and anxiety at the same time?

Yes. Many people feel both together, such as stress about a real deadline and anxiety about everything that might go wrong afterward.

How do I know if it is stress or anxiety?

A helpful question is whether the feeling is tied to a current, specific pressure. If it fades when the pressure lifts, it is likely stress. If it stays or has no clear trigger, it may be anxiety, and a professional can help you understand it.
