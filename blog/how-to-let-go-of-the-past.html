How to Let Go of the Past When It Still Feels So Close

By Lynette Wing, Clinical Homeopath, Naturopath, Mind-Body Practitioner · November 1, 2026

To let go of the past, start by understanding why it still feels close. Old pain often lives in the body as well as the mind, so your nervous system keeps replaying it to protect you. Letting go is not forgetting. It is gently teaching your body that what happened is over, and that you are safe now.

Why can't I let go of the past?

You may have told yourself a hundred times to move on. You know it is over. And yet a song, a smell, or a familiar street pulls you right back into the old moment, and your chest tightens as if it were happening again.

This is not weakness. Your body keeps an honest record of what you have lived through. When something hurt deeply, your nervous system filed it under "danger" so it could warn you if anything similar came near. The replaying is a faithful protector doing its job long after the job is done.

Here is the reframe I want to offer you: holding on is not a failure. It is a form of protection that has outlived its season.

What does it actually mean to let go?

Many people resist letting go because they think it means pretending the hurt did not matter. It does not.

Letting go is not forgetting. It is not excusing what someone did. It is not erasing the lessons you learned. Letting go means the past no longer gets to drive your present. You can remember it without being ruled by it.

Think of it like setting down a heavy suitcase you have carried through every room of your life. The contents are still real. You simply stop carrying them everywhere you go.

Why does the past feel stored in my body?

When an experience overwhelms us, the body often holds on to the feelings that never got to finish. You might notice it as a clenched jaw, a heavy chest, a stomach that drops, or a sense of bracing you cannot quite explain.

Sometimes the past we are carrying is not only our own. Families pass down unspoken rules like "Never let them see you hurt" or "Keep the peace at any cost." I call these survival scripts. They were written to keep someone safe, and they often show up today as habits like people pleasing. My post on how to stop people pleasing explores one of the most common scripts and the hidden love inside it.

How do I let go of the past and move on?

Letting go happens in small, safe steps. You cannot think your way out of something your body is still holding, so we begin with the body.

  1. The Naming: Say plainly what you are holding. "I am still grieving the friendship I lost." Naming a feeling helps your brain shift from alarm toward understanding.
  2. The Grounding Breath: When a memory rises, breathe in for four counts and out for six. Feel your feet on the floor. Remind your body: "That was then. This is now. I am here."
  3. The Unsent Letter: Write to the person, the younger you, or the situation. Say everything. Then decide whether to keep it, burn it, or tuck it away. The point is release, not delivery.
  4. The Present Anchor: Each day, notice three things in your life right now that are good or safe. This trains your attention to live where you actually are.
  5. The Gentle Return: When the old pain returns, and it may, do not scold yourself. Returning is not failing. It is part of how healing spirals forward.

If someone hurt you and forgiveness feels like the next hard step, my post on how to forgive someone who hurt you walks through it gently.

Is it normal to keep thinking about the past?

Yes. Everyone revisits the past sometimes, especially during transitions, anniversaries, or seasons of stress. It becomes worth extra support when the memories crowd out your days, disrupt your sleep, or keep you from the people and things you love.

The National Institute of Mental Health shares practical ways to care for your mental health and notes when it is time to reach out for help. If painful memories feel intrusive or overwhelming, please talk with your physician or a licensed mental health professional. If you are in crisis, call or text 988 in the United States.

Helping your body feel safe enough to release what it has been bracing against is the heart of my nervous system and emotional overwhelm support. We work slowly and kindly, at the pace your body can trust.

Pause for Reflection

Place your hand on your chest and take one slow breath. Bring to mind one thing from your past you keep carrying.

What has holding on to it been protecting you from? What might become possible if you set it down, even for one day?

"Remember ye not the former things, neither consider the things of old. Behold, I will do a new thing" (Isaiah 43:18-19). You are not defined by what happened to you. You are being made new.

FAQ

How long does it take to let go of the past?

There is no set timeline. Letting go usually happens in layers, and setbacks are part of the process, not proof that you are stuck.

Is letting go the same as forgiving?

They are related but not the same. Letting go is releasing the grip the past has on your present. Forgiveness is a specific choice about a person, and it can come later or alongside.

Why do old memories come back when I am stressed?

Stress puts your nervous system on alert, and an alert body scans for past danger. Old memories often surface more when you are tired, overwhelmed, or facing change.
