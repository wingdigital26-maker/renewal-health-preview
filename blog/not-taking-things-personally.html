How to Stop Taking Things Personally, and Why It Helps

By Lynette Wing, Clinical Homeopath, Naturopath, Mind-Body Practitioner · September 9, 2026

Most of what people do is not about you. Their sharpness, their silence, their bad mood may land on you without being caused by you. Learning to hear it that way is not denial. It is a shift that lowers your body's alarm and leaves your boundaries intact.

What does it mean that it is not about you?

It means that other people act out of what they are carrying, not out of a verdict they have reached about your worth. Their words may be aimed at you. They are rarely defined by you.

When someone answers with an edge in their voice, they are often responding to something older than the moment. You are standing in the light of a projector, and the film was loaded years ago.

Why do we take things personally in the first place?

Because the mind is built to look for our reflection in other people's faces, and because for many of us that scanning once kept the peace at home.

If you grew up reading the room to stay safe, then a furrowed brow is not a neutral event. That is not a character flaw. It is a survival skill that worked, and it has simply outlived the situation that trained it. The same instinct that made you attentive and kind is the one that now leaves you sore after ordinary conversations.

Their behavior is not a mirror showing your inadequacy. It is a window into their own landscape, their fears, their exhaustion, and their limits. When you stop hunting for your reflection in their eyes, you finally start seeing the person in front of you.

What is the second arrow?

The first arrow is the thing that actually happened, the remark or the cold reply. You cannot always avoid it. The second arrow is the one you fire into yourself afterward: they did that because I am too much, I am always the one people treat this way.

Taking something personally is mostly the second arrow. It turns a moment of friction into a lasting story about who you are. Noticing the difference lets you feel the sting of the first without building an identity out of it.

What happens in your body when you take something personally?

Your body treats a perceived personal attack as a threat, so it responds like one. The alarm centre of the brain takes the lead, stress hormones rise, breathing shortens, and the part of you that thinks clearly goes quiet for a while. That is why you can replay a two second interaction for two hours and feel worse each time.

When you name what is happening, this is their pain and not my worth, more of the work moves back to the thinking part of the brain. Curiosity is a physiological event, not only a nice attitude. A settled, curious state supports vagal tone, which is your body's own brake on alarm, and I have written about training that brake in vagal tone exercises for your hidden engine.

This is the same territory as being wired but tired, where the emergency has ended but the setting has not switched back. If that describes most of your days, nervous system and emotional overwhelm support is the work of teaching the system that the emergency is over.

Does this mean you have to tolerate bad behavior?

No. It usually works the other way around.

As long as you believe someone's anger is your fault, you will stay in the conversation trying to explain yourself out of it. Once you see that the anger belongs to their own history, leaving becomes simple. You can say, I can see you are hurting, and I am not going to continue while I am being shouted at.

Compassion explains the behavior. Boundaries decide how much of it enters your life. You can have a heart full of empathy and a front door that is firmly locked.

Proverbs 4:23 says to guard your heart, because everything you do flows from it. Guarding is not hardening. It is knowing what you carry home and what you leave at the door.

When should you talk to your doctor?

If low mood, anxiety, or a constant sense of dread is taking up most of your days, that deserves real evaluation rather than only a reframe. The same is true for sleep that will not come and for physical symptoms that keep returning. A perspective shift is a lens, never a substitute for care. For independent reading, the National Institute of Mental Health and the Cleveland Clinic health library are trustworthy places to start.

Pause for reflection

Put a hand somewhere on your chest. Let one breath out fully, slower than you took it in.

Then bring to mind the last comment that stung, and ask two questions without rushing the answers. What might this person have been carrying when they said it? And which arrow is still in me, the first one or the second?

You are not thin skinned. You are perceptive, and your perception has been working overtime without rest. FAQ

Is not taking things personally the same as making excuses for people?

No. Understanding where behavior comes from does not make it acceptable. It changes what you do next, because you stop trying to fix their reaction and start deciding what you will allow.

What if it really was about me?

Sometimes it is, and honest feedback is worth having. The test is proportion. If a comment about one thing you did becomes a verdict on your whole character, that is the second arrow talking.

Why do I feel this so physically?

Because social threat and physical threat share much of the same alarm circuitry. A cold reply can raise your heart rate the way a near miss in traffic does.
