What Is Alexithymia? When You Cannot Name What You Feel

Alexithymia is a personality trait marked by difficulty identifying and describing your own emotions. A person with it does have feelings, but may struggle to tell them apart or put them into words, and often notices them first as physical sensations. It is not a psychiatric diagnosis and it is not a character flaw.

What is alexithymia, in plain English?

Imagine being asked, "How do you feel about that?" and finding only a blank, or a tight stomach, where the answer should be. That is close to what many people with this trait describe.

A review published in the journal European Psychiatry and hosted by the National Institutes of Health describes alexithymia as a personality trait characterized by difficulties in identifying and expressing emotions, along with a practical, action-focused style of thinking. A second NIH-hosted review, in Frontiers in Psychology, calls it altered emotional awareness. In other words, the feelings are present. The awareness of them, and the language for them, is harder to reach.

What are the signs of alexithymia?

Drawing on those descriptions, it can look like:

  • Knowing you feel "bad" or "off" but not whether it is sadness, anger or fear
  • Struggling to describe feelings to people you love
  • Noticing emotion mainly through the body, such as a headache, a tight chest or an upset stomach
  • Preferring facts, tasks and details over talk about inner experience
  • Being told you seem distant when you do not feel distant at all

This list is for understanding, not for labeling yourself. Many of these are ordinary human experiences, especially in a hard season.

Is alexithymia a mental illness?

No. The European Psychiatry review states plainly that alexithymia is not a discrete psychiatric diagnosis. It is a trait, something that sits on a spectrum, with some people having a little of it and others much more.

That same review notes that it appears alongside a number of mental and physical health conditions. This is one reason I would gently encourage you not to settle the question alone. If naming your feelings has always been hard, or has become hard recently, a licensed mental health professional can help you understand what is going on. I do not diagnose.

How is alexithymia connected to autism?

Researchers have found a notably high overlap between alexithymia and autism. The Frontiers in Psychology review describes the relationship as complex and says its exact nature remains unclear.

Two things are worth holding together. Not every autistic person has alexithymia, and plenty of people with alexithymia are not autistic. And where the two do meet, it helps to explain a painful misunderstanding: a person can care deeply and still not be able to say so in the expected way. Lack of words is not lack of love.

If that sounds like you or someone in your home, you may find comfort in how I approach neurodivergent support. The goal is never to correct who a person is. It is to lower the load on the nervous system so that connection comes more easily.

How is alexithymia different from feeling numb?

They can look alike from the outside, but they are not the same thing.

  • Numbness is a dulling of feeling itself, often after a long stretch of overwhelm. I wrote about it in why do I feel emotionally numb.
  • Alexithymia is a difficulty recognizing and naming feelings that are there.

There is also a close cousin called interoception, your sense of what is happening inside your body. Because emotions arrive with physical signals, trouble reading those signals can make feelings harder to identify. You can read more in what is interoception.

Can you learn to name your feelings?

The honest answer is that the research is still young. The European Psychiatry review says there is little consensus so far on how best to help. So I will not promise you a result.

What I can share is how I think about it. A body that has spent years bracing often learns to turn the volume down on feeling, because feeling was not safe. The words do not come back under pressure. They come back under safety, slowly, the way a shy child steps out from behind a door.

A few gentle places to begin:

  • Start with the body. "My chest feels tight" is a complete and honest sentence.
  • Use simple pairs. Pleasant or unpleasant. Heavy or light. Warm or cold.
  • Borrow words. A printed feelings list or wheel gives you options to point at.
  • Go slowly. One check-in a day is plenty.
A gentle practice: set a quiet reminder for one moment each afternoon. When it sounds, finish this sentence without judging it: "Right now I am noticing..." A sensation counts. "Nothing" counts. You are simply opening the door.

In the work I do around emotional overwhelm and the nervous system, safety comes first and words follow. That work sits alongside your physician and any counselor you see, never in place of them. If you would like to read the research, here is the NIH-hosted review of alexithymia.

Pause for Reflection

Place a hand over your heart. Let your shoulders drop.

If your body could speak one sentence for you today, what might it say? Where in your body do you feel it?

"The Spirit itself maketh intercession for us with groanings which cannot be uttered" (Romans 8:26, KJV). You are understood even when you have no words. You were never required to explain yourself perfectly in order to be known.

It refers to difficulty identifying and describing your own emotions. The feelings are present, but the awareness of them and the words for them are harder to reach.

Is alexithymia a diagnosis?
