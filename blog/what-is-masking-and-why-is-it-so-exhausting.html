What Is Masking, and Why Is It So Exhausting?

By Lynette Wing, Clinical Homeopath, Naturopath, Mind-Body Practitioner · October 26, 2026

Masking is the ongoing effort of hiding or suppressing your natural traits, movements, or reactions so you appear more "typical" at work or in social settings. It takes real, sustained energy from your nervous system, which is why a day of masking can leave you far more drained than the day's actual tasks would explain.

What does masking actually look like?

Masking can be as visible as forcing eye contact that feels uncomfortable, or as quiet as rehearsing a sentence in your head before you say it out loud. It might mean suppressing a stim, like rocking or moving your hands, holding in a reaction to a noise or a light that is bothering you, or performing enthusiasm you do not feel so a conversation goes smoothly.

None of this is dishonesty. It is a learned survival strategy, often built over years, usually to avoid standing out, being misunderstood, or facing judgment. Your body learned it to keep you safe. That is not a flaw. That is faithfulness.

Why is masking so draining on your nervous system?

Your nervous system can only run so many processes at once. When part of its attention is constantly spent monitoring your face, your voice, your posture, and the room's reaction to you, there is less capacity left for the task in front of you, let alone for rest.

I often describe this as a hidden tax. The body stays in a low-level alert state for hours, even in settings that look calm from the outside. Nobody else sees the work, so nobody else understands why you come home empty. Over time, that sustained effort is a commonly described contributor to what many people call autistic or ADHD burnout, a depleted state that usually needs real rest and fewer demands to recover from.

If you have ever wondered why you are wiped out after a gathering you actually enjoyed, I wrote more about that in why you feel so drained after socializing. Masking is often a big part of that story.

What are the signs you might be masking?

Masking is so practiced for many people that they do not notice they are doing it. A few common messengers:

  • The crash: feeling far more exhausted after social time than the activity itself seems to explain.
  • The decompression need: needing significant alone time to come back to yourself after being around people.
  • The rehearsal loop: scripting conversations in advance or replaying them afterward, checking for mistakes.
  • The two selves: feeling like a different, more effortful version of yourself at work or in groups than you are at home.
  • The exhale: a wave of relief when you are finally alone or with people you do not have to perform for.

These are not character flaws. They are your body telling you how hard it has been working.

Can you learn to unmask safely?

Unmasking is rarely an all-or-nothing switch, and it does not need to be. Many people start small, allowing one suppressed trait back in one safe setting. That might look like letting a hand stim happen at home, or giving yourself permission to skip eye contact on a hard day.

Choosing where and with whom to unmask is a legitimate form of self-protection, not a failure of progress. Safety and context matter, and going slowly is not a lesser way to do this. A nervous system that has guarded you for decades needs to learn, gently and by experience, that it can stand down. If sensory input is a big part of your load, you may also find why you get overstimulated so easily helpful, and my broader post on nervous system support for neurodivergent adults walks through daily regulation ideas.

When should you talk to a professional?

If exhaustion, shutdowns, or overwhelm are affecting your daily functioning, or if you are seeking a formal evaluation for autism or ADHD, a physician or a qualified specialist is the right next step. This post is educational and is not a diagnostic tool. The National Institute of Mental Health is a reliable, independent place to read more about autism and ADHD.

FAQ

Is masking only something autistic people do?

No. It shows up for many neurodivergent people, including those with ADHD, and to a lesser degree in anyone adapting their behavior to fit in. It tends to be most costly and most sustained for people whose natural wiring differs more from what a setting expects.

Does masking mean someone is being fake?

No. It is a protective strategy, often learned early and reinforced by real social consequences. Seeing it as a survival skill rather than a character issue changes how you respond to it, in yourself or in someone you love.

Can masking contribute to burnout?

Sustained masking is a commonly described contributor to autistic and ADHD burnout, alongside other demands. If burnout is severe or ongoing, a physician or specialist familiar with neurodivergence can help you build a realistic recovery plan.

Is it possible to unmask completely?

For most people, unmasking is about choosing safer settings to be more fully themselves rather than removing every adaptation everywhere. Context still matters, and that is a reasonable way to approach it.
