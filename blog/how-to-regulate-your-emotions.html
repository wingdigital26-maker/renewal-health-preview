How Do You Regulate Your Emotions? A Body-First Guide

By Lynette Wing, Clinical Homeopath, Naturopath, Mind-Body Practitioner · September 30, 2026

You regulate your emotions by working with your body first, not by forcing your feelings away. Notice where the feeling lives, name it plainly, soften your breath so your nervous system can settle, and then choose your response. Regulation is not the absence of big feelings. It is the capacity to feel them without being swept away.

What does it mean to regulate your emotions?

Emotional regulation is your ability to experience a feeling, stay present with it, and respond rather than react. It is not suppression. Pushing emotions down does not make them disappear. It usually stores them in the body as tension, fatigue or a shorter fuse.

I often describe emotions as messengers. Anger may be telling you a boundary was crossed. Sadness may be telling you something mattered. Anxiety may be telling you your body does not yet feel safe. A messenger that is ignored tends to knock louder.

Your soul is the part of you that interprets. Every feeling passes through a storyteller within you that asks, "What does this mean? Am I safe? Am I loved?" Regulation gives that storyteller enough calm to answer with truth instead of old fear.

Why do my emotions feel so big and out of control?

Most of the time, it is not a willpower problem. It is a nervous system problem.

When your body shifts into its rapid-response gear, the part of your brain that plans and reasons goes quieter. Your heart rate rises, your muscles brace and your attention narrows to the threat. In that state, even a small comment can feel enormous.

Some of us also grew up in homes where feelings were not safe to show. If emotions were punished, mocked or simply ignored, your body may have learned to either explode or shut down, because nobody showed you the middle path. If the shut-down side feels familiar, my post on why you might shut down during conflict goes deeper.

This is not a flaw. It is a protective pattern, and patterns can be retrained.

How do you regulate your emotions in the moment?

Try this four-step rhythm the next time a feeling surges.

1. Notice it in your body. Before you think about the feeling, find it. A tight throat, a hot face, a clenched belly. Tenderness is not weakness; it is information.

2. Name it simply. Say it quietly or in your head: "This is anger." "This is grief." In a well-known study, researchers found that putting feelings into words reduced activity in the brain's alarm center, the amygdala. Naming is not wallowing. It is a way of turning the volume down.

3. Soften your breath. Breath is the nervous system's remote control. Breathe in gently through your nose and let the exhale be a little longer than the inhale. Let your shoulders drop and your jaw unclench. You are telling your body, "We are safe here. We can soften now."

4. Then choose. Once the wave has crested, ask, "What does this feeling need from me?" Sometimes it is a boundary. Sometimes it is rest. Sometimes it is simply to be felt and allowed to pass.

How do you build emotional regulation over time?

In-the-moment tools matter, but capacity is built in ordinary days.

  • Give your body regular safety. Consistent sleep, nourishing meals and slow mornings lower your baseline stress so feelings have more room to land.
  • Practice when you are calm. Breathing and naming are easier to reach for in a hard moment if you rehearse them in easy ones.
  • Reflect at the end of the week. Looking back on what stirred you and what softened helps your storyteller learn a new story. My journaling prompts for healing offer five gentle questions.
  • Borrow calm. Time with a steady, kind person helps your own body settle. We were designed to regulate together, not alone.

This is the heart of my emotional overwhelm and nervous system support: helping the body feel safe enough that emotions can move through instead of getting stuck.

When should you get extra support?

Please talk with your physician or a licensed mental health professional if your emotions regularly feel unmanageable, if you have panic attacks, persistent low mood, outbursts that frighten you or others, or thoughts of harming yourself. If you are in crisis in the US, you can call or text 988.

Pause for Reflection

Place one hand on your heart and one on your belly. Let your breath slow.

Ask yourself gently: What feeling has been knocking lately? What might it be trying to tell me?

"Be still, and know that I am God" (Psalm 46:10, NIV). Stillness is not the absence of feeling. It is the steady place from which feelings can be held.

You are not too emotional. You are learning to listen.

FAQ

Is it bad to cry or feel angry?

No. Every emotion carries information. Regulation is about how you respond to a feeling, not about getting rid of it.

How long does it take to get better at regulating emotions?

It varies for everyone. Most people notice small shifts first, like calming a little faster, long before big changes.

What is the difference between regulating and suppressing?

Suppressing pushes a feeling away. Regulating lets you feel it fully while staying grounded enough to choose your response.

Where can I start if I want support?

Try the four steps once this week and notice what changes. If you would like support, you can start a conversation through the contact page.
