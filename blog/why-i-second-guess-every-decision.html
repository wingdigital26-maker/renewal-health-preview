Why Do I Second-Guess Every Decision?

By Lynette Wing, Clinical Homeopath, Naturopath, Mind-Body Practitioner · September 20, 2026

Second-guessing every decision is usually a safety habit rather than a thinking problem. If being wrong once carried a real cost, your system learned to check, recheck, and seek permission before committing. Self-trust returns the same way it left, gradually, through small decisions you make and then leave alone.

What does chronic second-guessing look like?

It is quieter than people expect. Not agonising over a house purchase. The small stuff.

You draft the message, then rewrite it four times. You order, then wish you had ordered what she did. You make a sound call at work and spend the evening auditing it. You ask two friends what they would do, and when they disagree you feel relieved, because now the decision is open again and you do not have to own it.

The tiring part is not the deciding. It is the reopening. A decision that stays closed costs one moment of effort. A decision you keep reopening costs the whole week.

Why do I not trust my own judgement?

Because trusting yourself is not a belief. It is evidence, and somewhere along the line the evidence stopped being collected.

Self-trust is built when you make a call, live with the result, and find out you survived being wrong. If you grew up where a mistake brought disproportionate consequence, or where someone else's opinion always overrode yours, you never got to run that experiment. You learned the safer route: outsource the decision, delay it, or hedge it so nothing is fully yours.

There is often an inherited rule underneath. The perfectionist script says: if I am not perfect, I am not safe. Its hidden sentence is, "I will be so perfect that you cannot help but approve of me." That is a child's strategy for staying loved, and it is exhausting to run as an adult. The survival scripts we inherit walks through the whole family of these rules.

Is second-guessing anxiety or is it a habit?

Frequently both, feeding each other.

Here is the loop. Uncertainty raises a small alarm in the body. Checking lowers it, briefly. That relief teaches your nervous system that checking is what made you safe. So the next uncertainty brings a stronger urge to check, and your tolerance for an open question shrinks a little more.

That is why reassurance never quite lands. It eases the moment and strengthens the pattern underneath. The way out is not a better answer. It is a slightly longer pause before you go looking for one.

If the indecision has hardened into not moving at all, that is a different pattern with a different root, and why you feel stuck covers it.

How do I stop second-guessing myself?

By practising on decisions that do not matter, so the skill is there when one does.

Set the weight first. Ask what this decision costs if it is wrong. Most are reversible. Let the effort match the stakes.

Give it a deadline. Small decisions get five minutes. Medium ones get a day. The deadline protects you from the loop, not from the wrong answer.

Decide once, then close the file. When the reopening urge arrives, name it: "This is already decided." You are not suppressing a thought. You are declining an invitation.

Cut the polling in half. Ask one person, not four. More opinions dilute rather than clarify. Notice who you ask for information versus who you ask for permission.

Write down what you decided and why. A week later, read it back. This is how you rebuild the evidence file that was never kept.

Let a small one be wrong. Choose the wrong restaurant on purpose. Let your body discover that nothing happened.

What does self-trust feel like when it returns?

Not certainty. Certainty is not on offer, for anyone.

It feels like deciding and then thinking about something else. Like setting a boundary without the shame hangover afterwards. And like being wrong without it meaning anything about who you are.

Courage here often looks like tenderness. You do not have to feel strong to move forward. Moving while you still feel shaky is the braver version. "If any of you lacks wisdom, let him ask God, who gives generously to all without reproach" (James 1:5). Without reproach. You are allowed to not know yet and still be held. This is much of what I work with in emotional overwhelm and nervous system support, and how to stop taking things personally covers the related habit of reading yourself into other people's moods.

When should you talk to someone qualified?

Please speak with your physician or a licensed mental health professional if indecision is causing significant distress, if checking and reassurance-seeking take up large parts of your day, or if intrusive doubts feel impossible to resist. Those patterns have recognised names and effective care, and the National Institute of Mental Health describes obsessive-compulsive disorder and anxiety disorders clearly. Work like mine sits alongside that care, never in place of it.

Pause for reflection

Put both feet flat on the floor and feel the ground take your weight. Let one slow exhale go all the way out.

Ask yourself gently: whose approval am I waiting for before this decision counts as mine? And what would it feel like to make one small choice today and let it stand?

You are not indecisive. You were taught that being wrong was dangerous, and you are allowed to learn otherwise.

FAQ

Is overthinking decisions a sign of anxiety?

It can be one feature of anxious patterns, though many people who overthink are not anxious in a clinical sense. A licensed professional is the right person to assess your own picture.

Why do I feel regret even when the outcome was good?

Because the discomfort is attached to deciding, not to the result. That is a strong signal the pattern is about self-trust rather than judgement.

Does asking other people for advice make it worse?

Advice is useful. Polling for permission is not. The difference is whether you are gathering information or transferring responsibility.

Where should I begin?

Begin with one small decision today that you make and do not revisit. When you would like support shaped around your own history, you can start a conversation through the contact page.
