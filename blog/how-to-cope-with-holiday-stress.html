How Do You Cope With Holiday Stress? Gentle Ways to Stay Steady

By Lynette Wing, Clinical Homeopath, Naturopath, Mind-Body Practitioner · October 4, 2026

You cope with holiday stress by lowering the load on your nervous system before the season starts, not by trying harder once you are overwhelmed. Fewer commitments, steadier rhythms, honest expectations, and small moments of rest each day help your body stay out of survival mode, so you can actually be present for what matters.

Why are the holidays so stressful?

The holidays stack many stressors on top of each other. There is more to plan, more to buy, more to cook, and more people to please. Sleep gets shorter. Meals get later and sweeter. Routines that usually steady you fall away. And all of it comes wrapped in an expectation that you should feel joyful the whole time.

For many people, the season also stirs up old family dynamics and quiet grief. You may be walking into the same rooms, the same roles, and the same tensions you have carried for years. Your body remembers, even when you try not to.

So if you feel more tired, tense, or tearful this time of year, nothing is wrong with you. Your nervous system is responding honestly to a heavier load.

What happens in your body during holiday stress?

When demands pile up, your stress response switches on to help you meet them. That is good design for a short burst. But when it stays on for weeks, the body can slip into what I call "sympathetic lock," where being on guard becomes the default.

In that state, many people notice a tight jaw, shallow breath, an unsettled stomach, restless sleep, a short fuse, or cravings for sugar and quick comfort. The National Institute of Mental Health's stress fact sheet describes how ongoing stress can affect sleep, mood, and the body. These are messengers, not failures. They are your body saying, "This is a lot."

How can I prepare my nervous system before the holidays?

The kindest time to care for holiday stress is before December arrives. A few gentle moves now:

  1. Decide what matters most. Name the two or three moments you truly want to be present for. Let the rest become optional.
  2. Protect one anchor. Keep one daily rhythm no matter what: a morning walk, a quiet cup of tea, a set bedtime. One steady thing tells your body the ground has not disappeared.
  3. Plan your recovery, not just your events. For every big gathering, put a quiet morning or an early night on the calendar after it.
  4. Lower one standard. Store-bought dessert is fine. Fewer cards are fine. Good enough is a gift you can give yourself.

How do you handle stressful family gatherings?

You handle stressful family gatherings by going in with a plan for your body, not just your words. Before you arrive, take five slow breaths with a longer exhale than inhale. Decide in advance how long you will stay and what you will do if a familiar conversation starts to pull you under.

It helps to have a few small exits ready: stepping outside for fresh air, offering to wash dishes, or taking a short walk. These are not escapes. They are ways to let your nervous system reset so you can come back steadier.

If saying no to an invitation or a request makes you feel guilty, my post on how to set boundaries without guilt walks through why that guilt shows up and how to move through it. And if an old hurt is likely to be sitting at the table, how to forgive someone who hurt you may help you hold it more gently, without pretending it never happened.

What if the holidays bring up grief?

Grief often grows louder during the holidays. An empty chair, a tradition that has changed, or a season that simply looks different than you hoped can all ache. Grief is not something to manage away. It is love with nowhere to go.

Give it room. Light a candle, tell a story, or set aside a quiet moment to remember. You can hold sorrow and gratitude in the same heart. If the shorter, darker days are also weighing on you, why you may feel down in fall and winter explains how light and the body clock play a part.

When should you reach out for support?

Please talk with your physician or a licensed mental health professional if low mood, anxiety, or sleeplessness lasts for more than a couple of weeks, if you are leaning on alcohol or food to get through, or if grief feels unbearable. If you ever have thoughts of harming yourself, call or text 988 in the US.

Pause for Reflection

Set down whatever is in your hands. Let your shoulders fall and take one slow breath.

Ask yourself: What do I actually want to feel this season? What is one thing I can let go of so that feeling has room to grow?

When Martha was hurrying to prepare for her guest, Jesus said to her gently, "Martha, Martha, you are worried and upset about many things, but few things are needed" (Luke 10:41-42, NIV). He was not scolding her. He was inviting her to sit down.

You are allowed to be a guest at your own holidays too.

FAQ

Is it normal to feel sad during the holidays?

Yes. Many people feel a mix of joy, sadness, and tiredness during the holidays. If sadness lasts for weeks or makes daily life hard, please check in with your physician.

How do I stop holiday stress from affecting my sleep?

Keep your bedtime as steady as you can, dim the lights in the evening, and give yourself a few slow breaths before bed. Protecting sleep protects almost everything else.

How can I enjoy the holidays when I feel overwhelmed?

Shrink the season to what matters most. Choose a few meaningful moments, let go of the rest, and build in rest after every big day.
