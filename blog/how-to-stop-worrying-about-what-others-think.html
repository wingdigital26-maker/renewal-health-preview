How to Stop Worrying About What Others Think of You

By Lynette Wing, Clinical Homeopath, Naturopath, Mind-Body Practitioner · October 30, 2026

To stop worrying about what others think, start by understanding why it feels so urgent. For your nervous system, belonging once meant safety, so disapproval can register as danger. You ease the worry by calming your body, noticing the old story underneath, and anchoring your worth in something steadier than anyone's opinion.

Why do I care so much about what other people think?

Because you were made for connection. Long before you could reason, your body learned that being accepted meant being cared for. A frown, a sigh, or a silence could signal that something important was at risk. Your nervous system took notes.

For many of us, those notes became rules. Be agreeable. Do not be too much. Keep everyone comfortable. I call these survival scripts, family rules we absorbed to stay safe. They served a purpose once. The problem is that they keep running long after we have grown up, so a coworker's short reply can still feel like a threat to belonging.

That is why worry about other people's opinions rarely responds to logic alone. It lives in the body as much as the mind.

Is worrying about what others think a sign of anxiety?

It can be connected. Everyone cares what others think to some degree, and that is healthy. But when the worry is constant, when you replay conversations for days, avoid things you want to do, or feel sick before social situations, it may be part of a deeper anxiety pattern.

The National Institute of Mental Health describes social anxiety as an intense, persistent fear of being watched and judged by others that can interfere with daily life. If that sounds familiar, please talk with your doctor or a licensed mental health professional. You deserve real support.

If criticism in particular stings for a long time, my post on why you are so sensitive to criticism explores that piece more deeply.

How do I stop seeking approval from others?

Not by trying harder to not care. That just becomes another performance. Try these gentler steps instead.

  1. Notice the body first. When you feel that familiar tightening after a comment, pause and breathe out slowly. Your body needs to hear that you are safe before your mind can think clearly.
  2. Name the story. Ask, "What am I afraid this means about me?" Often the fear is old: that you are a burden, that you are not enough, that you will be left. My post on why you feel like a burden speaks to that one tenderly.
  3. Check the evidence. Most people are thinking about their own day, not judging yours. The harsh critic you imagine is often a voice from the past.
  4. Choose one small brave thing. Share an honest opinion, wear what you love, say no once. Your nervous system learns safety by experience, not by argument.
  5. Stop the comparison loop. Measuring yourself against others feeds the worry. Notice when you start keeping score, and gently set the scorecard down.

Where does real confidence come from?

From knowing who you are apart from anyone's applause. Approval is a moving target. Opinions shift with moods, seasons, and other people's wounds. If your worth rises and falls with them, you will always feel unsteady.

Shalom means nothing missing, nothing broken. It is a wholeness that lives inside you, not in your circumstances or in other people's reactions. You were designed on purpose. That truth does not change when someone misunderstands you. I wrote about this at length in my book, Nothing Missing, Nothing Broken, because I needed it too.

How can I get support with this?

If the worry about others has shaped your choices for years, you do not have to untangle it alone. In my nervous system and emotional overwhelm support, we work gently with both the body's alarm and the old stories beneath it, so you can show up as yourself without bracing.

Pause for Reflection

Think of one moment this week when you held back because of what someone might think. Do not judge it. Just notice it.

Ask yourself: If I were already fully accepted, what would I have said or done? What part of me is waiting for permission to be seen?

"I will praise thee; for I am fearfully and wonderfully made" (Psalm 139:14). You were known and loved before anyone formed an opinion of you.

FAQ

Is it normal to care what people think?

Yes. Caring about others is part of being human. It becomes a problem when the worry controls your choices or steals your peace.

Why do I replay conversations in my head?

Your mind is scanning for signs of rejection to keep you safe. It is protective, but it can become a loop that keeps your body on alert.

Can people-pleasing come from childhood?

Often, yes. Many people learned early that keeping others happy kept them safe. Those patterns can soften as your body learns new safety.
