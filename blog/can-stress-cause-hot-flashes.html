Can Stress Cause Hot Flashes? Why the Heat Rises When Life Does

By Lynette Wing, Clinical Homeopath, Naturopath, Mind-Body Practitioner · November 18, 2026

Stress does not cause menopause, but it can make hot flashes feel stronger and come more often. Cleveland Clinic lists feeling stressed as a common hot flash trigger and notes that stress can affect how intense and frequent they are. When your nervous system is already running hot, your body's thermostat has less room to stay steady.

What is a hot flash, and why does it happen?

A hot flash is a sudden wave of heat, often across the face, neck and chest, that can bring flushing, sweating and a racing heart. When it happens during sleep, we call it a night sweat. Many women first notice them in perimenopause, the years leading up to the final period.

Cleveland Clinic explains that as estrogen declines, the body's temperature control system can be disrupted. The brain's thermostat becomes more sensitive, so a small change can feel like an emergency, and the body rushes to cool itself down. That is not your body failing you. It is your body doing its best with a new set of signals.

Can stress trigger a hot flash?

Yes, for many women it can. Stress releases adrenaline and cortisol, the hormones that prepare you to act fast. Your heart speeds up, blood flow shifts, and your body warms itself for action. If your internal thermostat is already sensitive, that surge can be enough to tip it into a full hot flash.

I often describe the endocrine system as an orchestra. When cortisol plays at full volume for weeks, every other instrument has to adjust. Estrogen, progesterone and thyroid hormones all feel the strain. You can read more about that conversation in my post on whether stress can cause night sweats, which looks at what happens to your body while you sleep.

There is also a loop here. A hot flash in the middle of a meeting or a hard conversation can feel alarming, and that alarm releases more stress hormones. Learning to meet the heat with calm, rather than panic, can soften the cycle.

What else can make hot flashes worse?

Cleveland Clinic names several common triggers alongside stress:

  • Caffeine and alcohol
  • Spicy foods
  • Hot drinks like coffee, tea or hot chocolate
  • Hot showers, baths, warm rooms and hot weather
  • Heavy clothing or overheating during exercise
  • Smoking

Hot flashes are not only a menopause sign. Cleveland Clinic notes that thyroid disorders, infections and certain medicines can cause them too. If you are having hot flashes and you are not in perimenopause, that is a good reason to talk with your doctor.

How can you calm hot flashes during a stressful season?

The National Institute on Aging suggests noticing what triggers your hot flashes, because that knowledge helps you choose what to change. Here is how I like to weave their guidance into a gentle daily rhythm:

  • Cool your evenings. Lower the bedroom temperature, layer your bedding, and keep a fan and a glass of cold water close by.
  • Dress in layers. Something you can slip off at the first flush helps your body cool before the wave builds.
  • Lighten the triggers. Notice whether caffeine, alcohol or spicy foods line up with your worst days, and soften them for a few weeks to see.
  • Breathe low and slow. The NIA notes early research on mindfulness for hot flashes. Slow breathing tells your nervous system that you are safe, and a body that feels safe runs cooler.
  • Protect your margins. Fewer rushed mornings and overloaded afternoons give your thermostat less to react to.
A gentle practice: for two weeks, keep a simple note each time a hot flash arrives. Write the time, what you ate or drank, and what you were feeling just before. Patterns often appear quickly, and the note gives you and your doctor something real to work from.

When should you see a doctor about hot flashes?

Cleveland Clinic encourages you to contact your healthcare provider if hot flashes are interfering with your life. Please also see your doctor if they start when you are not near menopause, if they come with unexplained weight loss, fever or a racing heart at rest, or if night sweats are drenching. There are medical treatments, both hormonal and nonhormonal, and your physician can help you weigh them. You can read more in the National Institute on Aging guide to hot flashes and the Cleveland Clinic hot flashes page.

FAQ

Can anxiety feel like a hot flash?

Yes. A surge of anxiety can bring warmth, flushing and sweating that feel very similar. If the heat comes with other menopause changes, talk with your doctor about both.

Why are my hot flashes worse when I am stressed?

Stress hormones speed up your heart and warm your body. When your temperature control is already sensitive, that surge can make hot flashes stronger or more frequent.

Can you have hot flashes without menopause?

Yes. Thyroid problems, infections and some medicines can cause hot flashes, so it is worth asking your doctor if you are not in perimenopause.
