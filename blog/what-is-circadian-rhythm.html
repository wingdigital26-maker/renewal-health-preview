What Is Circadian Rhythm? Your Body's Daily Clock

Your circadian rhythm is the daily cycle your body keeps in step with day and night. The National Institute of General Medical Sciences describes circadian rhythms as the physical, mental and behavioral changes a living thing goes through over each day. They influence your sleep, hormone release, appetite, digestion and body temperature.

What is circadian rhythm, in plain English?

It is your body's sense of time. You get sleepy at night and alert in the morning. You get hungry around the same hours. Your temperature rises and dips on a schedule you never had to set.

The National Institute of General Medical Sciences, part of the National Institutes of Health, notes that most living things keep these rhythms, including animals, plants and even microorganisms. In people, nearly every tissue and organ has its own rhythm, and together they are tuned to the cycle of day and night.

Your body is not a machine that runs the same at every hour. It is a creature of morning and evening.

How does your body keep time?

With a system of biological clocks, led by a master clock in your brain. The Institute explains that this master clock is a group of nerve cells that keeps the clocks in your organs and tissues in step with each other.

It also guides the hormone melatonin, based on how much light your eyes take in. In the evening, your master clock tells your brain to make more melatonin, and you begin to feel sleepy.

What affects your circadian rhythm?

Light and dark have the biggest influence. The Institute also names food intake, stress, physical activity, your social environment and temperature.

I find that list comforting. It means the clock listens to ordinary things: when you see the sky, when you eat, when you move, who you sit with. None of them are complicated. All of them are daily.

What throws your body clock off?

The Institute gives a few common examples of a rhythm falling out of step with the outside world:

  • Travel across time zones, which we call jet lag
  • Shift work, which changes the usual pattern of sleeping and waking
  • Light from electronic devices at night, which can confuse the body's clocks

When this happens for a short time, you may feel drowsy, a little clumsy, and slow to learn or focus. The Institute also notes that long-term sleep loss and rhythms that keep shifting are linked with higher health risks and can make existing health issues worse.

If you work nights or care for someone through the night, please hear this without shame. You are not doing it wrong. Your body is working hard in a schedule it was not built around, and it deserves extra gentleness. If you often lie awake long after you want to be asleep, my post on why you can't fall asleep at night looks at that more closely.

How does circadian rhythm connect to hormones?

Hormone release is one of the functions the Institute lists as shaped by circadian rhythms. Melatonin is the clearest example, rising in the evening as the light fades.

This is why I ask about sleep and daily rhythm whenever someone comes to me about hormones. Your hormones are not only chemistry. They are also timing. A body that does not know what time it is has a harder time knowing what to release. I look at that whole picture in my hormone balance support, alongside your physician's care.

A gentle practice: tomorrow morning, step outside or stand at a bright window for a few minutes soon after you wake. No phone. Let your eyes take in the day. Then, as bedtime gets close, dim the lights and set your screens down. You are giving your clock its two oldest signals.

How can you support your circadian rhythm gently?

I do not believe in forcing the body into a schedule. I believe in giving it steady signals and letting it settle. The factors the Institute names point to simple places to begin:

  • Light: morning daylight, and softer light at night
  • Food: meals at roughly regular times
  • Movement: some activity during the day
  • People: unhurried time with those who feel safe to you

Choose one. Keep it for a week. Rhythm is built by returning, not by getting it perfect. If you would like a fuller picture, I wrote about a daily rhythm for body, soul and spirit, and my programs and packages are built around that same steady pace.

When should you talk to your physician about sleep and rhythm?

See your physician if poor sleep or daytime sleepiness is persistent, if it affects your safety while driving or working, or if it comes with other symptoms that worry you. Sleep trouble has many possible causes, and it deserves a proper evaluation. You can also read the full circadian rhythms fact sheet from the National Institute of General Medical Sciences.

Pause for Reflection

Place one hand on your heart. Take one slow breath in, and a longer breath out.

When did you last watch the morning arrive? What is the last thing your eyes see before sleep? What one signal could you give your body tomorrow that says, "It is day," or "It is night, and you can rest"?

"And the evening and the morning were the first day" (Genesis 1:5, KJV). Rhythm was written into creation before anything else was asked of us. Returning to it is a homecoming.

FAQ

Is circadian rhythm the same as your body clock?

They are closely related. Your biological clocks are the body's timekeeping system. Circadian rhythms are the daily changes those clocks produce, such as feeling sleepy at night and alert in the morning.

What controls circadian rhythm?

A master clock in your brain coordinates the clocks throughout your body. According to the National Institute of General Medical Sciences, light and dark have the biggest influence on it.

Can stress affect your circadian rhythm?

Yes. The Institute lists stress among the things that affect circadian rhythms, along with food intake, physical activity, social environment and temperature.
