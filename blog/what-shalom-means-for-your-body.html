Nothing Missing, Nothing Broken: What Shalom Means for Your Body

By Lynette Wing, Clinical Homeopath, Naturopath, Mind-Body Practitioner · September 8, 2026

Shalom is usually translated peace, which makes people picture a calm afternoon. The root, shalam, means something sturdier: to make whole, to restore, to complete, to bring back into alignment. Nothing missing, nothing broken. Read that way, wholeness stops being a mood you failed to maintain and becomes a structure that can be rebuilt.

What does shalom actually mean?

At its root, shalom describes a state where nothing is missing and nothing is broken. Not in your circumstances, which nobody can promise you, but in your internal structure. It is a word about integrity in the engineering sense: all the parts present, all of them connected, all of them doing the work they were designed to do.

That distinction matters, because most of us were taught to chase peace as a feeling. Feelings come and go with sleep and blood sugar and the news. Structure is different. Structure can be honestly assessed, and where something has come apart, it can be tended.

Why does wholeness show up in the body?

Because the body is where every belief eventually gets translated into biology. You are not a mind piloting a machine. Spirit, soul, and body were designed to work as one system, which is why an unresolved grief can show up as a jaw that will not unclench and why a season of relief can show up as digestion that finally settles.

That means your body is not the least spiritual part of you. It is the most honest. It reports what is actually happening rather than what you have decided should be happening, which is exactly why it is worth listening to. I wrote about that at length in why your body is the most honest witness you have.

What is the difference between survival and shalom?

Survival is not a failure state. It is an intelligent, expensive setting your body switches into when the environment asks it to. The trouble is that the setting outlives the emergency.

  • Survival contracts. Shalom expands.
  • Survival hides. Shalom reveals.
  • Survival protects. Shalom restores.

A body running survival keeps resources on the perimeter: alertness up, digestion down, repair postponed until later. It is a reasonable allocation during a genuine emergency and a costly one across a decade. Most of what gets labeled a stubborn symptom is really a body that never got the message that the emergency ended, which is the everyday territory of nervous system and emotional overwhelm support.

What does this change about how you read your symptoms?

It changes the question. Instead of what is wrong with me, the question becomes what is this protecting, and what has it not been told yet.

Your symptoms are not failures. Your patterns are not your identity. Your body is not your enemy and your mind is not betraying you. A symptom is a messenger, and a messenger that keeps knocking is usually one that has not been heard. That reframing is not softness for its own sake. It is the practical difference between fighting your own physiology and working with it, and bodies cooperate far more readily with the second approach. The mechanics of that are in chasing symptoms versus finding the root.

Where does faith fit without turning into pressure?

Gently, or not at all for now. Paul's blessing in 1 Thessalonians 5:23 asks that the whole person, spirit and soul and body, be kept well. Whole is the operative word. It is a blessing about integration, not about performance, and there is no version of it that requires you to feel a certain way before you are allowed to begin.

If none of that is your language, the physiology stands on its own and you are welcome here exactly as you are. This is an invitation, never an obligation.

When should you involve your doctor?

For anything physical, persistent, or worrying, start there. Fatigue, pain, mood changes, digestive changes, and sleep problems all deserve real evaluation, and a framework like this one is a lens for understanding rather than a substitute for care. If you want independent reading on how stress and the body interact, the National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health and the Cleveland Clinic health library are trustworthy places to look.

Pause for reflection

Let your shoulders drop. Let your jaw unclench. Then ask, without hurrying to answer: where in my life do I feel the absence of shalom? And what if that absence is not a flaw, but an invitation?

You are not behind. You are not late. This idea is the spine of my book, Nothing Missing, Nothing Broken, and it is the lens I bring to every conversation, including the first one. If you would like that conversation, reach out through my contact page and tell me where you are starting from.

FAQ

Is shalom just another word for feeling peaceful?

No. Peaceful is a mood. Shalom describes a structure where nothing is missing and nothing is broken, which is why you can be in a hard season and still be moving toward it.

Does this mean my symptoms are spiritual rather than physical?

No, and that split is the thing this framework is arguing against. Symptoms are physical, and they also carry information about the load a person is under. Both are true, and both deserve real attention.

Do I have to share your faith for this to be useful?

Not at all. The nervous system does not check anyone's beliefs before it responds to safety. Take the physiology and leave the rest if that is what fits.

Where do people usually start?

With the body, because a steadier body makes everything else easier to look at. That is why the first steps are usually practical and small rather than sweeping. The full sequence is laid out in body, soul, spirit and why the healing order matters.
