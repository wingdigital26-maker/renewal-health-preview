Why Do I Doom Scroll When I'm Already Exhausted?

By Lynette Wing, Clinical Homeopath, Naturopath, Mind-Body Practitioner · October 12, 2026

Doom scrolling is usually not a willpower problem. When your nervous system is stuck on alert, the endless feed offers small sparks of novelty that briefly distract you from discomfort without asking you to feel it. Seeing the habit as a stress response, not a character flaw, is the first gentle step toward putting the phone down.

What is doom scrolling, really?

Doom scrolling is the habit of continuing to scroll through upsetting, alarming, or simply endless content long after it stops feeling good. It often happens late at night, or in the middle of a hard day, and it usually leaves you more drained than when you started.

It is easy to call it a discipline problem. In my experience, it behaves more like a stress loop. The body feels unsettled, the phone offers a quick distraction, and the loop repeats, because the tension underneath never actually gets tended to.

You are not weak for doing this. Symptoms are messengers, and the late-night scroll is one of them.

Why do I reach for my phone when I'm overwhelmed?

When your nervous system senses threat, whether real danger or stress that never quite resolves, it looks for a way to make the discomfort smaller. The feed is always there, always new, and asks nothing of you. Each swipe offers a tiny shift in stimulation that can take the edge off for a moment.

But the relief does not settle the body. It only covers the activation for a while. That is why the exhaustion is still waiting the moment you set the phone down, and why the urge to pick it back up comes so quickly.

Why do I scroll at night when I'm so tired?

For many people, night is the first quiet moment of the day. The tasks stop, the house goes still, and every feeling that was pushed aside since morning finally has room to surface. Scrolling can be a way of not being alone with those feelings yet.

Sometimes it is also a quiet reach for time that feels like your own, when the rest of the day belonged to everyone else. That longing is real and worth honoring, but bright, urgent content right before bed tends to keep a tired body on alert rather than helping it wind down. If falling asleep has become a struggle, why you can't fall asleep at night looks at that side more closely.

What are the signs doom scrolling has become a stress response?

  • You reach for your phone the instant you feel bored, anxious, or unsettled
  • You keep scrolling even when the content makes you feel worse
  • You lose track of time and feel more depleted afterward, not less
  • It happens most at night, when your body is already asking for rest
  • Putting the phone down feels harder than picking it up did
  • You feel more on edge or snappish the next day

That last one matters. A nervous system that has been fed alarm late into the night often has a shorter fuse the next morning, and why you might feel so irritable lately explains how that builds.

How can I stop doom scrolling, gently?

Shame tends to feed the loop, so start with curiosity instead. Treat the urge as information rather than failure. A few small shifts tend to work better than an all-or-nothing phone ban:

  1. Pause and ask. When you feel the pull, ask your body what it actually needs right now: rest, food, water, a break, or simply permission to stop.
  2. Change the bedtime setup. Charge your phone outside the bedroom, or at least out of arm's reach.
  3. Give your body something steadier. Step outside for a few minutes of daylight, take three slow breaths with a long exhale, or name one calm thing you can see in the room.
  4. Swap, don't just stop. Keep a book, a journal, or soft music near the place you usually scroll.
  5. Be kind to yourself afterward. If you scroll anyway, notice it without judgment and begin again tomorrow.

When should I talk to my doctor or a therapist?

Please bring it to your physician or a licensed mental health provider if scrolling is interfering with your sleep, your relationships, or your ability to function, or if it comes with persistent anxiety or low mood. The Cleveland Clinic health library is a reputable place to read more about stress and sleep. If you ever have thoughts of harming yourself, call or text 988 to reach the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline.

You do not have to sort this out alone. If you are curious who you would be working with, you can read more about my story and approach.

Pause for Reflection

Set your phone face down. Rest a hand on your chest and take a slow breath in, then a long breath out.

Ask gently: What was I hoping to feel the last time I reached for my phone? What might give me that feeling more truly?

"Be still, and know that I am God" (Psalm 46:10). Your body was never built to take in an endless feed of urgency. Stillness is not a luxury. It may be exactly what you have been longing for.

FAQ

Is doom scrolling the same as phone addiction?

Not necessarily. It can overlap with compulsive phone use, but doom scrolling describes using the feed to manage a stressed or anxious nervous system, so the stress underneath is usually the more useful thing to address.

Will deleting social media apps fix doom scrolling?

It can reduce the trigger, but it does not settle the activation underneath. Pairing fewer triggers with real rest and calming habits tends to work better than removing access alone.

Can doom scrolling make anxiety worse?

It can. A nervous system that is already on alert often stays activated longer when it keeps taking in upsetting or urgent content, especially close to bedtime.
