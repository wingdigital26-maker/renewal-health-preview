What Is the Vagus Nerve? Your Body's Calming Pathway

By Lynette Wing, Clinical Homeopath, Naturopath, Mind-Body Practitioner · November 23, 2026

The vagus nerve is the main nerve of your parasympathetic nervous system, the part of you that handles rest and digestion. It runs from your brainstem down through your neck and chest to your gut, carrying signals between your brain, heart and digestive system. Cleveland Clinic notes there are two, one on each side of the body.

What is the vagus nerve, in plain English?

Think of it as the body's long line of communication between your head and your middle. Cleveland Clinic explains that "vagus" is the Latin word for wandering, and the name fits. It is the longest of your cranial nerves, and it takes a winding path from the lower brainstem through the neck, chest, heart and lungs, all the way to the digestive tract.

The work it does is involuntary. You do not decide to slow your heart after a scare or to start digesting lunch. This nerve helps carry those instructions for you, quietly, all day long.

What does the vagus nerve do?

According to Cleveland Clinic, the vagal nerves play a part in many automatic functions, including:

  • Digestion
  • Heart rate, blood pressure and breathing
  • Immune system responses
  • Mood
  • Saliva and mucus production
  • Speech and taste

That is a wide reach for one pathway. It is why a hard season can show up in so many places at once: a tight chest, a knotted stomach, a dry mouth, a voice that will not come out right. The signals travel the same road.

How does the vagus nerve help you feel calm?

Your autonomic nervous system has two sides. The sympathetic side prepares you for fight or flight. The parasympathetic side, which Cleveland Clinic describes as controlling "rest and digest" functions, brings you back down. The vagus nerve is the main pathway of that second side.

I often describe the nervous system as an internal security guard. When the guard decides you are safe, the vagus nerve acts like a gentle brake on the heart, and the body softens. Breathing slows. Digestion resumes. I call this the vagal brake.

When the guard has been on duty too long, that brake is harder to reach. This is not a character flaw. It is a body that has been protecting you faithfully. If that sounds familiar, I walk through the daily practices I teach in vagal tone exercises.

How is the vagus nerve connected to your gut?

Directly. The vagus nerve reaches the digestive tract and carries messages in both directions, which is one reason the gut and the brain seem to feel things together.

It helps explain why worry can turn your stomach, and why a settled meal eaten slowly can settle the rest of you. I wrote about this two-way conversation in the gut-brain connection. The body is not being dramatic when your stomach reacts to stress. It is being honest.

What are the signs of a vagus nerve problem?

Symptoms depend on the cause and on which part of the nerve is affected. Cleveland Clinic lists these possible signs:

  • Abdominal pain and bloating
  • Acid reflux
  • Changes to heart rate, blood pressure or blood sugar
  • Difficulty swallowing, or loss of the gag reflex
  • Dizziness or fainting
  • Hoarseness, wheezing or loss of voice
  • Loss of appetite, feeling full quickly, or unexplained weight loss
  • Nausea and vomiting

Please notice that this list overlaps with many other conditions. It is not something to sort out on your own. Fainting, trouble swallowing, a lasting change in your voice, or unexplained weight loss all deserve a prompt conversation with your physician, who can order the right testing. You can read the full Cleveland Clinic guide to the vagus nerve.

Can you stimulate the vagus nerve naturally?

There are two very different things people mean by this. One is medical vagus nerve stimulation, which Cleveland Clinic describes as an implanted device that a healthcare provider places and manages. That is a treatment decision between you and your medical team, and it is outside my work.

The other is simply living in ways that help your body feel safe: slow breathing, unhurried meals, humming or singing, time with steady people, real rest. I cannot promise what any one practice will do for you. I can say that the body softens under safety, not under pressure, and these are gentle ways to offer it safety.

A gentle practice: breathe in through your nose, then let the breath out slowly, as if you were fogging a window. Make the exhale a little longer than the inhale. Do this five times. You are not forcing calm. You are letting your body know it is allowed.

Cleveland Clinic explains that "vagus" is the Latin word for wandering. The nerve takes a long, winding course through the body.

Is the vagus nerve part of the rest-and-digest system?
