What Is the HPA Axis? Your Body's Stress Response System

The HPA axis is your body's main way of responding to stress. It is a communication loop between three organs: the hypothalamus and pituitary gland in your brain, and the adrenal glands on top of your kidneys. Together they release a chain of hormones that ends with cortisol, then signal each other to settle back down.

What is the HPA axis, in plain English?

HPA stands for hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal. Cleveland Clinic describes it as a communication system between three organs that creates a feedback loop of hormones to start and regulate your stress reaction.

I think of it as a relay. One runner hands the baton to the next, and the last one carries the message to the whole body. A thought, a sound, or a hard phone call can become chemistry in seconds.

What are the three parts of the HPA axis?

Each part has its own job:

  • The hypothalamus. A structure deep in the brain that keeps the body in balance, including temperature, hunger and mood.
  • The pituitary gland. A small, pea-sized gland at the base of the brain that makes several essential hormones and directs other glands.
  • The adrenal glands. Small, triangle-shaped glands that sit on top of each kidney and produce hormones, including cortisol.

How does the HPA axis release cortisol?

According to Cleveland Clinic, the chain reaction goes like this:

  1. In a stressful moment, your nervous system prompts the hypothalamus to release a hormone called CRH.
  2. CRH prompts the pituitary gland to release a second hormone, ACTH.
  3. ACTH prompts the adrenal glands to release cortisol.

Cortisol then brings the short-term changes that help you meet the moment. This is automatic.

The design also includes an off switch. Cleveland Clinic explains that rising cortisol tells the hypothalamus to stop sending the first signal, which ends the stress response. Your body was built to rise to a challenge and then return to rest.

What happens when the HPA axis stays switched on?

Stress itself is not the problem. Cleveland Clinic notes that the stress response can be positive, keeping us alert and ready to avoid danger. The trouble comes when the stress is frequent, intense, or never seems to end. Then the feedback loop can stop working as smoothly, and cortisol can stay raised. Cleveland Clinic states that long-term stress of this kind is linked with a higher risk of a range of health conditions.

I often describe the nervous system as an internal security guard. When the guard never gets to clock out, the whole relay keeps running. You may feel tired and alert at the same time, which is what I call "wired but tired." If that is your season, you are not weak. You are a person whose body has been protecting you faithfully for a long time.

Two honest notes belong here. First, Cleveland Clinic is clear that this is a complex area, that researchers are still studying it, and that there is no direct treatment for HPA axis dysfunction itself. Second, the axis can also become underactive. Cleveland Clinic explains that corticosteroid medicines may raise the risk of this, which is one reason those medicines should only ever be changed with your prescriber's guidance.

How can you support your stress response naturally?

Cleveland Clinic says that managing stress can help keep the HPA axis working as it should, and it lists everyday strategies:

  • Relaxation practices such as breathing exercises, yoga and muscle relaxation
  • Nutritious food, movement and enough sleep
  • Gratitude
  • Letting go of worry over what you cannot change
  • Learning to say no when you are already stretched
  • Staying connected with people who steady you

None of these is a quick fix, and I cannot promise what any one of them will do for you. What I can say is that the body softens under safety, not under pressure. I go deeper on the daily rhythms in how to lower cortisol naturally. If you are carrying someone else's care on top of your own, the signs of caregiver burnout may also sound familiar.

A gentle practice: tonight, choose one small signal that the day is over. Dim a lamp, wash your face slowly, or step outside for one minute of evening air. Say quietly, "The work of today is finished." You are giving the relay permission to rest.

In my practice, I look at stress alongside the rest of the picture, including hormone balance support and weight and metabolic health, always alongside your physician's care.

When should you see your doctor?

Please talk with your physician if you have lasting exhaustion, unexplained weight changes, dizziness, a low mood that will not lift, or frequent infections. These signs overlap with many conditions, and only proper testing can sort them out. You can read the full Cleveland Clinic guide to the HPA axis.

FAQ

What does HPA stand for?

HPA stands for hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal. It names the three organs in the loop: the hypothalamus, the pituitary gland and the adrenal glands.

What hormone does the HPA axis release?

Its main function is to release cortisol. Cleveland Clinic explains that two other hormones, CRH and ACTH, carry the signal along the way.

Is there a treatment for HPA axis dysfunction?

Cleveland Clinic states that there is no direct treatment so far and that researchers are still studying it. Managing stress is the main support it describes, and your physician can check for other causes.
