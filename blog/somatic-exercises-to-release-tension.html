Somatic Exercises to Release Tension in Your Body

By Lynette Wing, Clinical Homeopath, Naturopath, Mind-Body Practitioner · September 19, 2026

Somatic exercises release tension by speaking to your body in the only language it trusts: sensation rather than reasoning. Soften the jaw, drop the shoulders on a long exhale, breathe into your belly, hold yourself gently, and let rhythm move through you. Each one tells your nervous system the threat has passed.

What are somatic exercises?

Somatic exercises are small, deliberate physical practices that use posture, breath, touch, and movement to change how safe your body feels. Somatic means of the body.

They matter because tension is not stored in your opinions. It is stored in tissue. When the nervous system stays in protection mode, the body holds the shape of readiness: jaw set, shoulders lifted, breath shallow, belly guarded. You can decide a hundred times to relax and none of it reaches the jaw. A somatic practice goes the other direction. It changes the physical signal first and lets the mind follow.

The National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health has an overview of relaxation techniques and what the research shows. To be honest, the evidence base for body-based practices is growing but uneven, and none of this replaces medical care. What I can say is that these are safe, free, and repeatable.

Where does your body hold stress?

Usually in the same few places, without your permission.

  • The jaw. A clenched jaw mimics defensiveness. It is the last place people check and the first to give away that you are braced.
  • The shoulders. Physical bracing lives here. Shoulders hitched toward the ears is the posture of waiting for a blow.
  • The chest and breath. Shallow, upper-chest breathing is a survival pattern, usually noticed only when you finally sigh.
  • The belly. Digestion slows in protection mode, so a guarded stomach and unpredictable digestion travel with stress.

Find yours first. You cannot release what you have not noticed.

Which somatic exercises actually help?

These are the practices I hand people first. Each takes under a minute.

Unclench the jaw. Let your teeth part slightly and rest your tongue on the floor of your mouth. Exhale slowly. A soft jaw signals safety. Use when you feel stressed, defensive, or on edge.

Shoulder lift and release. Lift your shoulders toward your ears, hold three seconds, release on a long exhale. Repeat three to five times. Use when you feel tense or responsible for everything.

Belly breath. Place a hand on your belly. Inhale so your hand rises, then exhale slowly, until the breath deepens on its own. Deep belly breathing signals safety, where shallow breathing mimics survival. Use when you feel anxious or rushed.

The self-hold. Wrap your arms around yourself and hold gently for twenty to thirty seconds. Self-touch is associated with the body's bonding and settling chemistry. Use when you feel scared or need reassurance.

Gentle swaying. Standing or sitting, sway side to side slowly. Rhythmic movement mimics being rocked, a hardwired cue of safety. Use when you feel overwhelmed or grieving.

The open posture. Feet shoulder width apart, hands on your hips, chest and chin lifted slightly. Breathe deeply, rolling your shoulders back and down with each exhale, for thirty to sixty seconds. Expansive postures interrupt physical collapse. Use when you feel small or unseen.

How do you choose the right one in the moment?

Match the practice to what your body is doing, not what you wish it would do.

If you are tight and wound up, go for release: the jaw, the shoulders, the belly breath. If you are numb or far away, go for connection: the self-hold and the sway. If you are collapsed and small, go for the open posture. For a sharp spike of panic in a room full of people, grounding techniques for overwhelm covers the acute anchors, and tapping is a separate tool with its own sequence.

One rule matters more than the choice: repetition beats intensity. Sixty seconds a day teaches your nervous system more than an hour once a month, because the body learns safety through pattern rather than effort. To build that capacity over time rather than only in hard moments, vagal tone exercises is the longer game.

One more thing, for anyone who finds stillness difficult. Scripture says, "Be still, and know that I am God" (Psalm 46:10). Stillness is not idleness. For a body trained on urgency, it is the harder discipline.

What if a practice makes you feel worse?

Stop, and take that seriously rather than pushing through.

For some people, especially after trauma, turning attention inward can intensify distress rather than ease it. That is information, not failure. Try it with your eyes open, keep it shorter, or choose an external focus. And please work with a qualified professional if body-based attention consistently brings up more than you can hold.

Please also speak with your physician about persistent physical symptoms. Chronic jaw pain, chest pain, breathlessness, ongoing digestive trouble, or tension that does not respond to rest all deserve a proper evaluation. These practices support a body under care, they do not substitute for it. Where nervous system overwhelm is the pattern underneath, emotional overwhelm and nervous system support is where I work with it most closely.

Pause for reflection

Let your jaw soften. Drop your shoulders on your next exhale. Feel your feet on the floor.

Ask yourself gently: where am I still holding something I no longer need to carry? And what would it feel like to let that one place soften for sixty seconds, without earning it first?

Your body is not your opponent here. It is your most honest witness, and it has been waiting to be asked.

FAQ

How long does it take for somatic exercises to work?

Many people feel something shift within a minute or two, though a lasting change in baseline tension builds with practice. Choose one or two that match where your body holds tension, and keep them brief and daily rather than long and occasional.

Can somatic exercises release trauma?

Body-based practices can help a nervous system settle, and settling often makes processing possible. Trauma work itself belongs with a qualified trauma-informed professional.

Where should I begin?

Begin with the jaw and the exhale. They are available anywhere. When you would like support shaped around your own body, start a conversation through the contact page.
