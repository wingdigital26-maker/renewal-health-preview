What Is Interoception? Your Body's Inner Sense

By Lynette Wing, Clinical Homeopath, Naturopath, Mind-Body Practitioner · November 22, 2026

Interoception is your awareness of the signals coming from inside your body. It is how you know you are hungry, thirsty, cold, in pain, or that your heart is beating fast. Cleveland Clinic describes it as a learned skill that develops as you grow, and one that comes more easily to some people than others.

What is interoception, in plain English?

Most of us were taught about sight, hearing, touch, taste and smell. Interoception is a sense that faces inward. It tells you how you feel on the inside.

Cleveland Clinic gives everyday examples: a full bladder, hunger, thirst, feeling hot or cold, nausea, muscle tension, itchy skin, pain, nervousness, and the feeling of your own heartbeat.

It also shapes emotion. Their example is a simple one. You are in a dark room and your heart is pounding, so you feel scared. Because you noticed, you can do something about it. You turn on the light.

How does interoception work?

Cleveland Clinic lays it out in three steps:

  1. Sensing. A sensor in your body picks up a signal and sends it along a nerve to your brain. Your stomach rumbles.
  2. Interpreting. Your brain decodes the signal into something you can understand. You feel hungry.
  3. Integrating. You read the message and decide what to do. You eat.

When you answer the signal, your body returns to balance and the message goes quiet. This is the body doing what I have always believed it does. It tells the truth, and it waits to be heard. I wrote about that in your body is the most honest witness you have.

Why do some people struggle to read their body's signals?

Because noticing a signal and understanding it are two different skills. Cleveland Clinic describes them this way:

  • Attention: how often you notice signals from your body
  • Accuracy: how correctly you understand them

Some people notice everything and cannot sort it. Others notice very little until the signal is shouting. It varies a great deal from person to person.

Cleveland Clinic lists some signs that this inner sense may not be working as expected:

  • Trouble knowing when you need the bathroom
  • Not feeling hungry or thirsty, or not feeling full after a meal
  • A very high or very low tolerance for pain
  • Feeling anxious easily
  • Difficulty managing emotions

If you saw yourself in that list, please do not read it as a flaw. Many of us learned early to override our bodies. Finish your plate. Stop crying. Push through. A body that was not listened to learns to speak more quietly. That quiet can feel like emotional numbness, and it is often protection, not absence.

How does interoception relate to autism and ADHD?

Cleveland Clinic explains that with neurodivergence, interoception may be heightened or may not work as expected. It offers two examples:

  • Autism: accuracy can be low. It may be hard to identify bodily needs like hunger, or to name and express emotions.
  • ADHD: attention to signals can be strong, yet acting on them can be hard. You feel thirsty and still cannot decide what to drink.

This helps explain things families often take personally. The child who does not notice they need the bathroom until it is urgent. The adult who forgets to eat all day and then falls apart at dusk. That is not carelessness. It is a signal that arrived late or arrived tangled.

I do not diagnose autism, ADHD or any other condition, and questions about a diagnosis belong with a qualified specialist. What I offer through my neurodivergent support work is gentle, whole-person care for the nervous system, alongside your medical and therapy team. Brains are wired differently, not incorrectly.

Can you improve interoception?

Yes, it can be practiced. Cleveland Clinic lists deep breathing exercises, mindfulness, yoga, meditation and cognitive behavioral therapy, and notes that a healthcare provider or mental health professional can help you find what fits your situation.

They also say something I love: have patience with yourself, because this skill takes time and does not come naturally to everyone. You can read their full guide to interoception.

A gentle practice: set a quiet reminder for three times tomorrow. Each time, pause and ask your body three questions. Am I thirsty? Am I hungry? Is anything tight? You do not have to fix what you find. Noticing is the whole practice.

Why does interoception matter for eating and everyday care?

Because so much of daily care depends on catching a signal in time. Hunger, fullness, thirst, tiredness and the need to rest are all inner messages. When they are faint or confusing, eating and resting can turn into guesswork, and guesswork is tiring.

This is one reason I begin with the body before anything else. In my weight and metabolic health support, we start with safety and listening before any rules.

If the signals you are missing include pain, thirst, hunger or bathroom needs in ways that affect your health or safety, please talk with your physician.

Pause for Reflection

Place one hand on your heart and one on your belly. Take a slow breath.

What can you feel right now? A heartbeat, warmth, hunger, nothing at all? Whatever you notice, or do not notice, let it be allowed.

"Be still, and know that I am God" (Psalm 46:10, KJV). Stillness is where the quiet signals become audible. You are not behind. You are learning to listen.

FAQ

What is an example of interoception?

Feeling your stomach rumble and knowing you are hungry is interoception. So is noticing a full bladder, a racing heart, thirst, or tight shoulders.

Is interoception something you are born with?

Cleveland Clinic describes it as a learned skill that develops as you grow. That is good news, because a skill can be practiced at any age, at your own pace.

What are signs of poor interoception?

Cleveland Clinic lists difficulty knowing when your bladder is full, difficulty managing emotions, feeling anxious easily, a high or low pain tolerance, and not feeling hungry, thirsty or full.
