Can Stress Cause a Fever? Understanding Stress and Body Temperature

By Lynette Wing, Clinical Homeopath, Naturopath, Mind-Body Practitioner · October 25, 2026

Yes, for some people stress can raise body temperature. Researchers call this psychogenic fever, and it usually shows up as a low-grade warmth or flushing during or after intense stress. Because a fever is much more often a sign of infection or another illness, any fever deserves a physician's attention before you assume it is stress.

How can stress raise your body temperature?

Your body keeps its temperature in a narrow, careful range, a little like a thermostat in a home. When the nervous system senses danger, it releases stress hormones and shifts energy toward protection. In some people, that alarm response also nudges the thermostat up.

Researchers have studied this for years. A review in the journal Temperature describes psychogenic fever, a rise in core temperature linked to psychological stress, which can be short and sharp in a stressful moment or a lower, lingering warmth during a long season of strain. It appears to be driven by the stress response itself rather than by infection.

I sit with many people who tell me they feel "hot and wrung out" by the end of a hard week. They are not imagining it. A body that has been bracing for a long time is working hard, and it often shows.

Can anxiety cause a low-grade fever?

Some people notice a temperature that runs slightly high on anxious days, or a warm, flushed face before a hard conversation. Others feel chills and shivers when stress peaks, which can feel like the start of a flu. If your hands also shake when you are on edge, my post on why stress can cause shaking explains that side of the alarm response.

Night-time warmth can be part of the picture too. Some people wake up damp and overheated during stressful seasons, even when they feel well by morning.

Can stress make you sick with a real fever?

Stress can affect the immune system, so sometimes the fever is not from stress directly but from an infection that found a tired body. Many people notice they come down with something right after a deadline or a holiday. I explore that pattern in why you get sick when you are stressed.

This is why it matters to let your physician look first. A fever can come from viral or bacterial infections, inflammatory conditions, medications, and many other causes. The MedlinePlus page on fever gives a clear overview. Natural-first support works beautifully alongside that care, never instead of it.

What helps your body cool down from stress?

Think of these as gentle signals of safety, offered alongside any care your physician recommends.

  1. Slow the out-breath. Breathe in for a count of four and out for a count of six. A longer exhale tells your body the danger has passed.
  2. Hydrate steadily. Sip water through the day, especially if you feel warm or have been sweating.
  3. Cool the body kindly. A cool cloth on the back of the neck or wrists, lighter layers, and fresh air can help you feel more comfortable.
  4. Rest without guilt. A warm, tired body is asking for rest. Listening is not weakness.
  5. Track what you notice. Write down your temperature, the time of day, and what was happening. That record helps you and your physician see the pattern.

When should I see a doctor about a fever?

Please see your physician for any fever that lasts more than a couple of days, keeps returning, or comes with a rash, a sore throat, pain when you urinate, a cough, or unexplained weight loss. Always call your doctor about a fever in an infant, an older adult, someone who is pregnant, or anyone with a weakened immune system.

Seek emergency care right away for a fever with a stiff neck, a severe headache, confusion, trouble breathing, chest pain, a seizure, or a rash that does not fade when pressed.

Ask gently: What has my body been working so hard to hold together? Where could I let a little of that heat and effort go today?

FAQ

Can stress cause a temperature without being sick?

For some people, yes. Researchers describe psychogenic fever as a temperature rise linked to stress rather than infection. Because fever has many other causes, please have your physician check it first.

Can anxiety cause chills and hot flashes?

Yes. The stress response can bring waves of heat, flushing, or chills. Hot flashes can also be linked to hormone changes and other conditions, so mention them to your physician.

How long does a stress fever last?

It varies. Some people feel a brief rise during a stressful moment, while others notice a lower warmth during long stressful seasons. Any fever that lingers should be evaluated by your doctor.
