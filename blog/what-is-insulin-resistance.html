What Is Insulin Resistance? A Gentle, Plain-English Guide

By Lynette Wing, Clinical Homeopath, Naturopath, Mind-Body Practitioner · November 20, 2026

Insulin resistance is a condition in which your body does not respond to insulin the way it should. Insulin is the hormone that helps move sugar from your blood into your cells. The National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases explains that when cells stop responding well, blood sugar can rise over time.

What does insulin do in your body?

Insulin is a hormone made by your pancreas. After you eat, sugar from your food enters your bloodstream, and insulin helps that sugar move into your cells, where it becomes fuel.

I think of insulin as a messenger knocking on the door of each cell. "Open up. Dinner is here." When everything is working well, the door opens, the fuel goes in, and your blood sugar settles.

What is happening when cells stop listening?

With insulin resistance, the knock is still there, but the doors open slowly. The National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases describes it this way: cells in your muscles, fat and liver do not respond well to insulin. Over time the pancreas may not be able to make enough insulin to keep up, and more sugar stays in the blood.

The same source says insulin resistance can lead to higher blood sugar levels and weight gain. This is one reason I never treat stubborn weight as a character flaw. If you have ever felt that your weight will not budge no matter how hard you try, there may be a real conversation happening between your hormones and your cells.

You are not failing. Your body is working harder than you know.

What are the signs of insulin resistance?

Often there are none. According to the National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases, people with insulin resistance usually have no symptoms at all.

That is why it is unwise to guess from how you feel. An afternoon slump or a strong pull toward sweets can have many explanations, and stress is often one of them. A feeling is worth noticing. It is not a test result.

What causes insulin resistance?

Researchers do not fully understand what causes it. The National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases lists several things that make it more likely, including:

  • Carrying extra weight, especially around the waist
  • Not being physically active
  • A family history of diabetes
  • Getting older
  • Smoking
  • Certain health conditions, such as polycystic ovary syndrome and sleep apnea
  • Certain medicines taken over time

Some of these you cannot change, and none of them are a verdict on you. They are simply part of your story. Stress belongs in the picture too. I have written about how stress can raise your blood sugar and how it may nudge cholesterol as well, because the body under pressure keeps extra fuel in the blood on purpose.

How do doctors check for insulin resistance?

Most people are surprised to learn that doctors may not test for insulin resistance directly. The National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases notes that the test for it is used mainly in research. Instead, your doctor may order simple blood tests that show whether your blood sugar is higher than normal, and may also check cholesterol and triglycerides.

So if you are wondering about this, the next step is not a supplement or a strict plan. It is a conversation with your physician and a blood test. Only your doctor can tell you what your numbers mean, and whether medicine has a place in your care. You can read more on the NIDDK insulin resistance and prediabetes page.

How can you support your body gently?

The National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases points to a healthy lifestyle as the foundation, and names four parts of it:

  • Healthy foods and drinks
  • Regular physical activity
  • Managing your weight
  • Getting enough sleep

It also says something I love: making these changes can be hard, and you may want to start with small ones. That is permission to go slowly.

In my practice I add one layer underneath. A body that feels unsafe holds on. Sleep, steady meals, and a calmer nervous system are not extras. They are the ground everything else stands on. The body heals under safety, not pressure.

A gentle practice: after one meal a day this week, take a slow ten-minute walk. You do not need a pace or a tracker. Let it be a way of telling your body that it has been fed and it is safe to use what it was given.

This is the whole-person work I describe on my weight and metabolic health page. It sits alongside the care you receive from your physician, never in place of it.

FAQ

Can you have insulin resistance and not know it?

Yes. The National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases says people with insulin resistance usually have no symptoms. Blood tests ordered by your doctor are the way to find out where your blood sugar stands.

Is insulin resistance the same as diabetes?

No. Insulin resistance means your cells do not respond well to insulin. It can lead to higher blood sugar over time, and for some people that may progress. Your doctor can explain where you are and what to watch.

Does stress affect blood sugar?

It can. Stress hormones signal the body to release stored fuel into the blood. That is helpful in a short emergency and harder on the body when the stress never lets up.
