How to Forgive Yourself When You Feel Stuck

By Lynette Wing, Clinical Homeopath, Naturopath, Mind-Body Practitioner · October 2, 2026

To forgive yourself, you name what happened honestly, take whatever responsibility is truly yours, make amends where you can, and then stop punishing yourself for it. Self-forgiveness is not pretending it did not matter. It is choosing to stop living as your own prosecutor, so your body and soul can finally soften and grow.

Why is it so hard to forgive yourself?

It is hard because the part of you that keeps replaying the mistake believes it is protecting you. Somewhere along the way, it learned that if you stay hard on yourself, you will never make that mistake again.

I see this often. People who would extend endless grace to a friend hold themselves to a standard they would never ask of anyone else. They replay the conversation at 2 in the morning. They rehearse the apology they already made. They believe that letting go would mean they did not care.

That inner critic is not evil. It is a survival script, usually learned early, often handed down. But it was never meant to run your life.

What is the difference between guilt and shame?

Guilt says, "I did something wrong." Shame says, "I am something wrong." That difference changes everything.

Healthy guilt is useful. It points at a specific action and invites repair. Once the repair is made, guilt has done its job and can leave.

Shame does not leave. It attaches to your identity and keeps whispering long after the moment is over. It sounds like "This is just who I am," or "I always ruin things." And as long as you believe a pattern is simply who you are, it stays off-limits for change.

One of the gentlest shifts I teach is replacing "What is wrong with me?" with "What is this moment inviting me to notice?" Curiosity is the antidote to shame.

What does self-blame do to your body?

Self-blame keeps the nervous system on guard. I like to say it this way: criticism is a stressor, and compassion is a regulator.

When you are constantly bracing against your own judgment, your body stays in a low hum of threat. Shoulders creep up. Breath stays shallow. Sleep gets lighter. Over time, that steady pressure can leave you tired, tense and emotionally overwhelmed.

Researchers who study self-compassion, like those gathered by the Greater Good Science Center, describe it as treating yourself with the same kindness you would offer someone you love. It is not self-pity, and it is not an excuse. It is what makes real change possible, because a body that feels safe is a body that can learn.

If your nervous system feels stuck on high alert, my emotional overwhelm and nervous system support work focuses on helping the body soften first, so the soul has room to heal.

How do you actually forgive yourself?

You move through it in small, honest steps. There is no single moment of release. It is a practice you return to.

  1. Name it plainly. Write down what happened, without softening it and without exaggerating it. Facts only.
  2. Separate the action from your identity. "I spoke harshly to my daughter" is true. "I am a terrible mother" is shame talking.
  3. Take your true share. Ask what part was yours, and what part belonged to circumstances, other people, or what you did not yet know.
  4. Repair what you can. An apology, a changed habit, a kind act. Repair turns guilt into growth.
  5. Speak to yourself as you would to a friend. Out loud, if it helps. "You were doing your best with what you knew. You are learning."
  6. Release it again when it returns. It will. That is not failure. Returning is the skill.

The American Psychological Association describes forgiveness as a process rather than a single decision, and that is just as true when the person you are forgiving is you.

Is forgiving yourself different from forgiving someone else?

In many ways it is harder, because you cannot walk away from yourself. You live with the one who hurt you. But the heart of it is the same: releasing the debt so you are no longer bound to it. I wrote about the other side of this in how to forgive someone who hurt you, including why forgiveness never requires you to excuse what happened.

When should you get extra support?

If guilt or shame feels constant, if you cannot stop replaying a past event, or if you have thoughts of harming yourself, please reach out to a licensed mental health professional. If you are in crisis, call or text 988 in the United States to reach the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline any time.

Pause for Reflection

Place a hand over your heart and take one slow breath out.

Ask yourself gently: What am I still punishing myself for? What would I say to a dear friend who carried the same thing?

"Therefore, there is now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus" (Romans 8:1, NIV). You were never meant to carry a sentence that has already been lifted.

You are not your worst moment. You are the one learning to grow from it.

FAQ

Is it selfish to forgive yourself?

No. Self-forgiveness usually makes you kinder and more present with others, because you are no longer spending your energy on self-punishment.

What if I do not feel like I deserve forgiveness?

That feeling is very common, and it is often shame speaking. Forgiveness is not earned. You can begin before you feel ready.

How long does it take to forgive yourself?

There is no set timeline. Many people find it comes in layers, with the weight getting lighter each time they return to the practice.
