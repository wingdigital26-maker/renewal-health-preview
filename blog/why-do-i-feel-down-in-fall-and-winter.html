Why Do I Feel Down in Fall and Winter?

By Lynette Wing, Clinical Homeopath, Naturopath, Mind-Body Practitioner · October 3, 2026

Many people feel lower in fall and winter because shorter days bring less light, and light helps set the body's internal clock, sleep, energy, and mood. Add holiday pressure and less time outdoors, and the body can feel heavier. For some, it is a mild seasonal dip. For others, it is a real condition that deserves care.

Why do shorter days affect my mood?

Your body keeps time by light. Morning light helps tell your internal clock when to wake, when to feel alert, and, later, when to wind down. When the days shorten and the mornings stay dark, that signal gets weaker.

The result can feel like a body that is out of step with itself: sleepy in the afternoon, wide awake at the wrong times, and heavier in mood than the circumstances explain. Nothing is wrong with you for feeling it. You were created to live in rhythm with the seasons, and you notice when they change.

What is the difference between the winter blues and seasonal depression?

The winter blues are a mild, common dip: a little more tired, a little less motivated, more drawn to staying in. Most people still function well and feel lighter on sunny days.

Seasonal affective disorder, often called SAD, is a form of depression that follows a seasonal pattern. It is more than a low mood. It can include persistent sadness, losing interest in things you usually enjoy, sleeping much more, pulling away from people, and feeling hopeless. The National Institute of Mental Health explains seasonal affective disorder and the treatments available.

If what you feel sounds more like the second description, please talk with your physician. It is real, and it is treatable.

Why do I crave carbs and sleep more in winter?

Many people notice they want more comfort food and more sleep as the light fades. When energy dips, the body often looks for quick fuel, and heavier, sweeter foods can feel soothing in the moment. Darker evenings also tell the body to wind down earlier.

These are not failures of discipline. They are signals. Your body is asking for energy, warmth, and comfort. The kindest response is to meet those needs in steadier ways: regular meals with protein, warm nourishing food, and a bedtime that honors the season without sleeping the whole day away. I write more about the link between stress, appetite, and energy in my weight and metabolic health support.

If you wake up tired even after a full night, my post on waking up tired after a full night's sleep may help you look a little deeper.

How can I care for myself when the days get darker?

Gentle, steady habits help the body find its rhythm again. Try a few of these:

  1. Get outside in the morning. Even a cloudy morning gives your body more light than indoors. Ten minutes on a walk or the porch with your coffee counts.
  2. Keep a steady wake time. A consistent morning anchors the rest of the day, even on weekends.
  3. Move your body daily. Gentle movement lifts energy and helps you sleep at night.
  4. Plan small warmth on purpose. A call with a friend, a candle at dinner, music while you cook. Put something to look forward to on the calendar.
  5. Watch the holiday load. You do not have to say yes to everything. A rested heart enjoys the season more.
  6. Stay connected. Winter can quietly isolate us. Reach out before you feel like it.

Some people find light therapy boxes helpful for seasonal depression. Because they are not right for everyone, especially with certain eye conditions or medications, that is a conversation to have with your physician first.

When low seasons tangle with stress and overwhelm, it can help to look at the whole picture. That is part of my emotional overwhelm and nervous system support. If you want a simple place to start, my free guide offers gentle first steps.

When should I see my physician?

Please talk with your physician or a licensed mental health professional if low mood lasts most days for more than two weeks, if you have lost interest in things you love, if sleep or appetite has changed significantly, or if you feel hopeless. Your physician can also check for other causes of fatigue and low mood. If you ever have thoughts of harming yourself, call or text 988 in the US, any time of year.

Pause for Reflection

Look toward the nearest window and notice the light, however much there is.

Ask yourself: What does my body need from me in this season? Where could I let winter be a time of rest instead of a time of pushing?

"He has made everything beautiful in its time" (Ecclesiastes 3:11, NIV). Even the quiet, darker months have their own kind of beauty, and their own invitation to slow down.

You are not falling behind. You are wintering. And spring always comes.

FAQ

When does seasonal low mood usually start?

For many people it begins in late fall as the days shorten and eases in spring. Some notice it starting as early as September or October.

Can seasonal low mood happen in summer?

It can, though it is less common. If your mood reliably drops in any season, it is worth mentioning to your physician.

Does getting outside really help?

Morning daylight helps your body keep time, and many people feel steadier with a daily walk outdoors. It supports, but does not replace, care for seasonal depression.
