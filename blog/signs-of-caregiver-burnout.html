Signs of Caregiver Burnout: When Caring for Others Empties You

By Lynette Wing, Clinical Homeopath, Naturopath, Mind-Body Practitioner · November 18, 2026

Caregiver burnout is deep physical and emotional exhaustion that builds when you care for a loved one with little rest or support. Common signs include feeling overwhelmed, isolated, tired most of the time, easily irritated, and losing interest in things you once enjoyed. The Office on Women's Health notes caregivers report much higher stress than non-caregivers.

What is caregiver burnout?

A caregiver is anyone who looks after someone in need: an aging parent, a spouse who is ill, a child with a disability, a friend after an injury. The Office on Women's Health explains that many family caregivers are "on call" almost all day, managing medicines, meals, appointments and decisions on top of their own work and family.

Caregiver burnout is what happens when that giving runs on empty for too long. It is not a lack of love. Most caregivers I meet love deeply. It is a nervous system that has been braced for months or years without enough time to come back down.

What are the signs of caregiver burnout?

The Office on Women's Health lists signs of caregiver stress that are worth taking seriously:

  • Feeling overwhelmed most days
  • Feeling alone, isolated, or deserted by others
  • Sleeping too much or too little
  • Gaining or losing a lot of weight
  • Feeling tired most of the time
  • Losing interest in activities you used to enjoy
  • Becoming easily irritated or angered
  • Feeling worried or sad often
  • Having headaches or body aches often

You might also notice small things first. Forgetting your own appointments. Eating standing up. Feeling a flash of resentment and then a wave of guilt for feeling it. These are not signs that you are a bad caregiver. They are signs that you are a human one.

Why do caregivers ignore their own needs?

Many of us were raised with an unspoken rule that everyone else comes first. In my post on survival scripts and inherited family rules, I call this the martyr script: the belief that love means emptying yourself completely. It looks noble, and often it was learned for good reasons, but it leaves no one to care for the caregiver.

There is also the simple reality of time. When someone depends on you, your own needs feel like they can wait. The trouble is that the body keeps an honest record. Sleep, digestion, mood and immunity all carry the cost of that waiting.

How does caregiving stress affect the body?

When you are always listening for the call, the bell, or the fall, your nervous system stays in a state of readiness. Stress hormones keep you alert, but over time they can wear on sleep, appetite, mood and energy. The Office on Women's Health notes that women caregivers may be less likely to get regular screenings, enough sleep, or regular physical activity.

If you are caring for a neurodivergent child or family member, the load can carry its own layers of advocacy, sensory planning and constant adjustment. My page on neurodivergent support speaks to families in that season, and my post on how to recover from burnout looks at the deeper work of rebuilding.

How can you care for yourself while caring for someone else?

Self-care for caregivers has to be small enough to actually happen. Here are a few places to begin:

  • Let others give you a break. The Office on Women's Health encourages reaching out to family, friends or a local resource. Name one specific task someone else could take.
  • Protect one daily anchor. A ten-minute walk, a slow cup of tea, a short prayer. Something that is only yours.
  • Keep your own appointments. Your checkups matter as much as theirs.
  • Lower the bar on everything that is not essential. Paper plates are allowed.
  • Talk about it. A friend, a support group, a counselor. Isolation makes everything heavier.
A gentle practice: tonight, write down three things you did today for your loved one, and one thing you did for yourself. If the second list is empty, choose one small thing for tomorrow and put it on the calendar like any other appointment.

My page on emotional overwhelm and nervous system support explains how I walk alongside people who are carrying a heavy load.

When should a caregiver get help?

Please talk with your doctor if signs of stress are lasting, if you are sleeping very little, or if sadness or worry is not lifting. Local caregiver programs and respite care can give you real time off. If you ever have thoughts of harming yourself, call or text 988 in the US, or call 911 in an emergency. You can read more on the Office on Women's Health caregiver stress page.

Pause for Reflection

Place one hand on your heart. Breathe in slowly, and let the breath out even more slowly.

Who is caring for you right now? What is one thing you could ask for this week without apologizing?

"Bear ye one another's burdens" (Galatians 6:2, KJV). That verse runs both ways. You are allowed to be the one who is carried for a while.

FAQ

What is the difference between caregiver stress and caregiver burnout?

Caregiver stress is the strain of caring for someone. Burnout is what can follow when that stress goes on for a long time without rest, leaving deep exhaustion and a sense of emptiness.

Is it normal to feel resentful as a caregiver?

Yes. Resentment often signals that your own needs have gone unmet for too long. It does not cancel your love. It is a messenger asking for support.

How do I ask for help with caregiving?

Be specific. Ask one person for one task, such as a meal, a ride, or two hours of company for your loved one, so it is easy for them to say yes.
