Can Stress Cause Shingles? What Your Body Is Telling You

Stress does not cause shingles by itself. Shingles comes from the chickenpox virus waking up after years of resting quietly in your nerve cells. But the National Institute on Aging notes that stress can weaken your immune system for a short time, and a weakened immune system is one of the things that can make shingles more likely.

What is shingles, and where does it come from?

Shingles is a painful skin rash caused by the varicella-zoster virus, the same virus that causes chickenpox. The National Institute on Aging explains that after you recover from chickenpox, usually as a child, the virus continues to live in some of your nerve cells. For many adults it stays inactive for life. For others, it becomes active again years later.

You cannot get shingles unless that virus is already in your body. Stress may simply be one of several things that lower the guard.

Can stress really wake the virus up?

Researchers do not fully understand why the virus becomes active in some people and not others. The National Institute on Aging names two things that make it more likely: older age, and trouble fighting infections. Illness, certain medicines and long-term health conditions can all weaken the immune system. The same page adds that even stress or a cold can weaken it for a short time.

This matches what I see again and again. The body rarely speaks up in the middle of the crisis. It speaks afterward, when the adrenaline fades and there is finally room to feel how much was carried. If you have noticed that you get sick when you are stressed, the pattern is the same one. Your body was not failing you. It was spending everything it had to get you through.

What are the first signs of shingles?

Shingles usually shows up in a small area on one side of the body or face, most often as a band around one side of the trunk. The National Institute on Aging lists these common symptoms:

  • Burning or shooting pain
  • Tingling, itching, or numbness of the skin
  • Fluid-filled blisters
  • Chills, fever, headache, or upset stomach

The first sign is often a burning or tingling feeling before any rash appears. That is different from stress-related itchy skin without a rash, which tends to move around and come and go. It is also different from cold sores that show up in hard weeks, which come from a separate virus and sit around the lips.

When should you see a doctor about shingles?

Quickly. This is not a wait-and-see rash. The National Institute on Aging says to see your doctor no later than three days after the rash starts, because early treatment with antiviral medication can help the blisters clear faster and limit severe pain.

See your doctor right away if you notice blisters on your face. Blisters near or in the eye can cause lasting eye damage. You can read the full guidance on the National Institute on Aging shingles page, including information about the shingles vaccine, which is worth asking your doctor about.

Nothing I offer replaces that visit. My role comes alongside your medical care, never in place of it.

How can you support your body while it recovers?

Once you are under your doctor's care, the invitation is simple: stop pushing. The National Institute on Aging suggests several comfort measures, and they are gentle ones:

  • Wear loose-fitting, natural-fiber clothing
  • Apply a cool washcloth to the blisters
  • Get plenty of rest and eat well-balanced meals
  • Try light movement, such as stretching or walking, with your doctor's approval

It also advises avoiding stress as much as possible, because stress can make the pain worse. I would say it this way. The body heals under safety, not pressure. Recovery is not the week to catch up on everything you missed.

A gentle practice: twice a day, sit somewhere quiet and breathe in for a count of four, then out slowly for a count of eight. Do this five times. You are not trying to fix anything. You are telling your nervous system that the emergency is over.

What is the stress underneath trying to say?

Shingles often arrives after a long season of holding everything together. A move. A loss. Months of caregiving. The rash is the virus, but the timing is often a messenger.

When the acute phase has passed, it is worth asking what your body had been carrying before it spoke up. That is the kind of whole-person work I describe on my inflammation support page: looking gently at sleep, nourishment, rhythm and the load your nervous system has been under, alongside the care you receive from your physician.

FAQ

Can stress alone give you shingles?

No. Shingles only happens when the chickenpox virus already living in your nerve cells becomes active again. Stress may weaken your immune system for a short time, which is one of several things that can make that more likely.

Is shingles contagious?

You cannot catch shingles from someone else. However, the National Institute on Aging notes that direct contact with fluid from the rash can spread the virus and cause chickenpox in someone who has never had chickenpox or the vaccine. Keep the rash covered and wash your hands often.

Can shingles come back?

Most people have shingles only once, but the National Institute on Aging says it is possible to have it more than once. Your doctor can talk with you about lowering that risk.
