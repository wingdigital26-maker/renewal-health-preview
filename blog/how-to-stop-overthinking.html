How Do You Stop Overthinking? A Body-First Guide

By Lynette Wing, Clinical Homeopath, Naturopath, Mind-Body Practitioner · October 4, 2026

You stop overthinking less by arguing with your thoughts and more by helping your body feel safe enough to let them go. Looping thoughts are usually a braced nervous system searching for certainty. When the body softens, the mind has less to guard, and the loop begins to loosen on its own.

What is overthinking, really?

Overthinking is when your mind circles the same thought without arriving anywhere. It comes in two shapes. One looks backward: replaying a conversation, a mistake, a look on someone's face. The other looks forward: rehearsing every way tomorrow could go wrong.

Both feel like problem-solving. But problem-solving moves toward a next step, and overthinking moves in a circle. If you have had the same thought forty times and are no closer to an answer, you are not solving. You are guarding.

Why does my mind keep looping?

Your mind keeps looping because some part of you believes that if you just think hard enough, you will finally be safe. The loop is not a character flaw. It is a protector.

When the nervous system is stuck in what I call "sympathetic lock," the stress response stays switched on as the default. A body in that state scans for danger, and the mind follows, refusing to rest until every gap is closed. The trouble is that life always has gaps. So the search never ends.

I see this often in the women I work with. They are not weak. Their bodies have learned that vigilance keeps everyone safe.

Why can't I just tell myself to stop?

You cannot simply tell yourself to stop because the part of you that loops does not take orders from logic. The nervous system listens to experience: breath, posture, tone of voice, the felt sense of the room. You can know with your whole intellect that the email was fine, and your body can still be braced as if it were not.

That is why trying harder often makes it worse. Fighting a thought tells the body there is a threat. The kinder path is body first, and let the mind follow.

How do you stop overthinking, step by step?

Start small. Choose one or two and practice them for a week.

  1. Notice the loop and name it. Say quietly, "I am looping." Naming moves you from inside the storm to standing beside it. You are no longer the thought. You are the one noticing it.
  2. Lengthen your exhale. Breathe in for a count of four and out for a count of six, five or six times. A longer exhale is one of the clearest signals of safety you can send your body.
  3. Drop your shoulders and unclench your jaw. Racing thoughts usually live in a gripping body. Release the grip and they often slow too.
  4. Give the worry a container. Set aside ten minutes, write every looping thought on paper, then close the notebook. Your mind can let go more easily when it trusts the thought has somewhere to live.
  5. Ask, "Is there a next step?" If yes, take it, even a tiny one. If no, this is a thought to set down, not solve.
  6. Come back to your senses. Feel your feet on the floor. Name three things you can see. The present moment is the one place a loop cannot follow.

For more in-the-moment anchors, my post on grounding techniques for overwhelm walks through several. And if you wonder why progress feels slow, how long nervous system healing takes explains why the body is often the last to believe the good news.

Why is overthinking worse at night?

Overthinking is often worse at night because the day's distractions fall away and the body is finally still. For a nervous system used to being on guard, stillness can feel unfamiliar, and unfamiliar can feel unsafe. So the mind fills the quiet with work to do.

A gentle wind-down helps: dim the lights, set the phone in another room, and breathe slowly before you lie down. If a thought arrives, jot it on a notepad and tell yourself, "I will look at this in the morning."

When should you talk to a professional?

Please speak with your physician or a licensed mental health professional if your thoughts feel constant or intrusive, if they are affecting your sleep, work, or relationships, or if you notice persistent worry, panic, or low mood. The National Institute of Mental Health describes the signs of anxiety disorders and the help available. If you ever have thoughts of harming yourself, call or text 988 in the US.

Pause for Reflection

Place a hand on your chest and let your next exhale be slow and long.

Ask yourself: What am I trying to protect with all this thinking? What would it feel like to trust that I do not have to have every answer tonight?

"And the peace of God, which transcends all understanding, will guard your hearts and your minds in Christ Jesus" (Philippians 4:7, NIV). Notice the word guard. You were never meant to be the only one standing watch.

You are not broken for thinking so much. You are a careful heart that is learning it can rest.

FAQ

Is overthinking a sign of anxiety?

It can be. Repetitive worry is common with anxiety, but it also shows up during ordinary stress. If it is constant or distressing, a licensed professional can help you understand what is going on.

Can overthinking affect your body?

Many people notice tension, shallow breathing, poor sleep, or an unsettled stomach when their thoughts race for long stretches. Any persistent physical symptom deserves a check with your physician.

What is the fastest way to stop a thought loop?

There is rarely a fast switch, but a long, slow exhale and coming back to your senses can soften a loop within a few minutes for many people.
