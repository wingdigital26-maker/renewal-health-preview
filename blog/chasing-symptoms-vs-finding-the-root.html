Chasing Symptoms vs Finding the Root: What Root-Cause Really Means

By Lynette Wing, Clinical Homeopath, Naturopath, Mind-Body Practitioner · August 24, 2026

← Back to the blog

If you have ever treated one symptom only to have another one show up somewhere else, you already understand the limits of chasing symptoms. Root-cause work asks a different question. Instead of "what medication or product handles this symptom," it asks "what is actually happening underneath that produced it in the first place."

Misleading is not the same as meaningless. A symptom is still a report worth reading, which is the case made in your body is the most honest witness you have.

That is not a rejection of medicine. It is a different lens layered alongside it, one that looks for the pattern behind the symptom instead of only the symptom itself.

What does "root cause" actually mean?

Root-cause thinking starts from the idea that the body does not usually create random, disconnected problems. A skin issue, a digestive complaint, and a sleep problem can all trace back to the same underlying imbalance, showing up in different places because the body is one connected system, not a set of separate parts.

Symptom management asks, "how do we quiet this specific complaint?" Root-cause work asks, "what is the body responding to, and why is it responding this way right now?" Both questions matter. They are just not the same question.

Why symptoms alone can be misleading

A symptom is information, not the whole story. It tells you the body is reacting to something, but not automatically what that something is. Two people with the same symptom can have very different underlying pictures, which is part of why a one-size-fits-all approach so often falls short.

This is where patience matters. Root-cause work usually takes longer to unfold than a quick fix, because it is looking for the actual source rather than the fastest way to make a symptom quiet down. That trade-off is worth naming honestly, because it is real.

My own experience with this

For twelve years, I lived inside a body that felt unpredictable and overwhelmed, after a lupus diagnosis sent me looking for answers that symptom management alone was not giving me. It took real time and real work before I felt safe and regulated in my own body again, for the first time in over a decade.

That season is exactly why I do this work the way I do it now. I am not interested in guessing at symptoms. I am interested in understanding what your body is actually telling you and why.

What root-cause work looks like in practice

A root-cause approach usually means slowing down long enough to ask a fuller set of questions: what has changed in the body over time, what patterns keep repeating, what has already been tried, and what has not been asked yet. It draws on naturopathic and homeopathic principles to look at the whole person rather than an isolated complaint.

This is complementary work, not a replacement for your physician. A root-cause approach and conventional medical care can and should work together, especially for anything that needs diagnosis, monitoring, or medication management. If something in your body concerns you, your doctor is always the right first call.

FAQ

Is root-cause work the same as alternative medicine that replaces my doctor?

No. It is complementary support that works alongside your physician, your medication plan, and the rest of your medical care, not a replacement for any of it. Think of it as an additional layer, not a substitute.

Does finding the root cause mean symptoms stop right away?

Not usually. No responsible practitioner promises specific outcomes on a set timeline. Root-cause work tends to unfold gradually, since it is addressing patterns rather than silencing a single symptom.

Who is root-cause work a good fit for?

People who have tried addressing individual symptoms without lasting change, or who want to understand the "why" behind what their body is doing, often find this framework useful. It is educational and supportive, not a diagnostic replacement for medical evaluation.

Pause for reflection

There is a kind of relief in stopping the chase, in setting down the exhausting work of putting out one fire after another and asking instead what is actually burning underneath. Scripture offers a similar invitation in Psalm 139:23-24, where the psalmist asks to be searched and known, not patched over. Your body deserves that same honest, patient attention.
