How Do You Deal With Impostor Syndrome, Gently?

By Lynette Wing, Clinical Homeopath, Naturopath, Mind-Body Practitioner · October 8, 2026

You deal with impostor syndrome by treating it as a safety signal, not a verdict on your ability. Name the feeling when it rises, let your body settle before you argue with the thought, collect real evidence of your growth, and stop measuring yourself against other people's highlights. Most of all, let your worth rest on something deeper than performance.

What is impostor syndrome?

Impostor syndrome is the persistent feeling that you are not as capable as people think, and that sooner or later you will be found out. It is not a formal diagnosis. It is a pattern of thinking and feeling that shows up in high achievers, caregivers, new leaders, and anyone stepping into a bigger room than the one they grew up in.

Researchers describe the impostor phenomenon as common across professions and stages of life, often alongside perfectionism and fear of failure. You are far from alone in it.

What I notice most is this: the feeling rarely matches the facts. People with long track records carry it. The evidence is right there, and still the body braces.

Why do I feel like a fraud when I am doing well?

Because success can feel like exposure. When you rise, more people see you, and to a nervous system shaped by survival, being seen can register as danger. The old script says, "It is safer to stay small." Promotion, praise, or a new opportunity quietly breaks that rule, and the body sounds the alarm.

So the fraud feeling is often not a thought problem at all. It is your nervous system doing its faithful job of protecting you from a risk that no longer exists. The body is not lying to you. It is simply working from an old map.

As a homeopathic practitioner, I have spent years sitting with people whose bodies carry patterns that neither conventional medicine nor talk therapy could fully reach. Impostor feelings are one of those patterns. They often began long before the job or the role that now triggers them.

What does impostor syndrome feel like in the body?

It is not only in your head. Many people notice:

  • A tight chest or held breath before they speak in a meeting
  • A flush of heat when someone praises them
  • Over-preparing late into the night, unable to call the work finished
  • A quick deflection ("Oh, it was nothing") that leaves before the compliment can land
  • A crash of exhaustion after a "win," instead of satisfaction

These are signs of a body in protection mode. Listening to them with curiosity rather than criticism is the first shift.

How do I stop comparing my inside to everyone else's outside?

Comparison feeds impostor feelings faster than anything. You see the polished outcome of someone else's work and set it beside your own unfinished, uncertain process. That is never a fair contest.

Try catching the comparison in the moment and naming it: "I am measuring my rough draft against their final copy." I wrote more about this pattern in how to stop comparing yourself to others, including why the body treats comparison as a status threat.

What actually helps with impostor syndrome?

Gentle, consistent practices tend to work better than pep talks:

  1. Settle first, then think. When the fraud feeling spikes, slow your breath with a longer exhale and feel your feet on the floor. Arguing with a braced body rarely wins.
  2. Name it without obeying it. "This is the impostor feeling again." Naming creates a little space between you and the story.
  3. Keep an evidence file. Write down what you actually did, learned, and handled. Facts are steadier than feelings.
  4. Let one compliment land. Next time someone thanks you, say only "Thank you," and breathe before you move on.
  5. Separate worth from output. Your work can improve. Your worth was never up for review.

That last one sits at the heart of my book, Nothing Missing, Nothing Broken, which explores what it means to live from wholeness rather than proving. Scripture puts it plainly: "For we are His workmanship" (Ephesians 2:10). Workmanship is not something you earn. It is something you already are.

If impostor feelings are part of a bigger pattern of overwhelm, my services page explains the ways I support the whole person, body first, at a gentle pace.

When should I talk to a professional?

If self-doubt is constant, keeps you from work or relationships, or comes with ongoing low mood, anxiety, or trouble sleeping, please talk with your physician or a licensed counselor. Those are signs your body and mind deserve more support, not that you are failing.

Pause for Reflection

Place a hand over your heart and let your shoulders drop.

Ask gently: When did I first learn that being seen was not safe? What would change if I believed I belong in the rooms I have been invited into?

You are not a fraud waiting to be caught. You are a person still learning that you are allowed to be here.

You were made for this room.

FAQ

Is impostor syndrome a mental illness?

No. It is not a formal diagnosis. It is a common pattern of self-doubt. If it comes with persistent anxiety or low mood, talk with a qualified professional.

Who gets impostor syndrome?

Almost anyone can, and it is common in high achievers, people in new roles, and those who are the first in their family to reach a certain place.

Does impostor syndrome ever go away?

For many people it softens with time, practice, and a more settled nervous system. It may still visit during big transitions, and that is normal.
