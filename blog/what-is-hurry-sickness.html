What Is Hurry Sickness? Why You Cannot Slow Down, Even When You Want To

By Lynette Wing, Clinical Homeopath, Naturopath, Mind-Body Practitioner · October 15, 2026

Hurry sickness is a popular term for a constant sense of urgency: rushing, multitasking, and feeling anxious about time even when nothing is actually due. It is not a medical diagnosis. It often describes a nervous system that has been on alert so long that speed feels safer than stillness.

What does hurry sickness mean?

The phrase describes a way of living more than a condition. You speed through tasks, interrupt yourself, finish other people's sentences, and feel restless the moment you sit down. Even rest gets rushed, scheduled, and measured.

I see this so often in caring, capable people. They are not careless or frantic by nature. They learned somewhere along the way that being fast meant being safe, needed, or good enough. Hurry became the way the body says, "If I keep moving, nothing bad can catch up with me."

What are the signs of hurry sickness?

Every person is different, but common signs include:

  • Feeling impatient in lines, traffic, or slow conversations
  • Doing several things at once and finishing few of them well
  • Eating quickly, often standing or at a screen
  • Feeling guilty or uneasy when you are not being productive
  • Racing thoughts at bedtime, replaying the day or planning tomorrow
  • Forgetting small things because your mind is always one step ahead

If several of those feel familiar, you may also relate to feeling guilty for resting, which often travels right alongside hurry.

Why can't I slow down even when I want to?

Because hurry is often not a choice. It is a state. When your nervous system has lived in survival mode for a long time, slowing down can feel strangely threatening. Stillness gives the feelings you have been outrunning a chance to catch up, so the body speeds up again to avoid them.

Over time, that state can affect more than your schedule. The American Psychological Association describes how ongoing stress can touch nearly every system in the body, from muscles and breathing to digestion and sleep. Many people with hurry sickness notice tight shoulders, shallow breath, a rushed gut, and trouble winding down at night.

This is why I never tell anyone to simply "slow down." Your body needs to be shown, gently and repeatedly, that slowness is safe. My post on how to get out of survival mode goes deeper into that shift.

How do you recover from hurry sickness?

Recovery is less about one big change and more about small moments of practiced slowness.

  1. Single-task one thing a day. Wash one dish, drink one cup of tea, or read one page with nothing else running.
  2. Slow your exhale. Breathe in for a count of four and out for a count of six. A longer exhale is one of the simplest ways to signal safety.
  3. Build margin into your calendar. Leave space between commitments so you are not always arriving late in your own body.
  4. Pick the longest line, on purpose. Then breathe and notice what rises in you. It is a small, honest practice in patience.
  5. Eat one meal sitting down. No screen, no rush. Let your body taste its food.
  6. Keep a weekly rest rhythm. Sabbath is not a reward for finishing everything. It is part of how we were designed.

When is constant rushing a sign of something more?

Sometimes hurry is linked to anxiety, attention differences such as ADHD, burnout, or sleep problems. Please talk with your physician or a licensed counselor if restlessness and racing thoughts are wearing you down, affecting your sleep, or making daily life hard to manage. If you are having thoughts of harming yourself, call or text 988 to reach the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline.

Pause for Reflection

Set a timer for two minutes. Sit, put both feet on the floor, and do nothing at all.

Notice what happens. Does your body want to get up? Do your thoughts start making lists? Ask gently: Who taught me that I had to hurry to be safe? What might I hear if I let myself be still?

"In returning and rest shall ye be saved; in quietness and in confidence shall be your strength" (Isaiah 30:15). Slowness is not falling behind. It is coming home.

FAQ

Is hurry sickness a real condition?

It is not a formal medical diagnosis. It is a widely used term for a pattern of chronic rushing and time pressure that many people recognize in themselves.

Can hurry sickness affect your health?

Constant rushing often comes with ongoing stress, and long-term stress can affect sleep, digestion, mood, and muscle tension. Your physician can help if symptoms concern you.

How long does it take to stop rushing?

It varies. Most people notice small shifts within weeks of practicing slowness daily, and deeper change as the nervous system learns over time that it is safe.
