Prebiotics vs Probiotics: What Is the Difference?

By Lynette Wing, Clinical Homeopath, Naturopath, Mind-Body Practitioner · November 23, 2026

Probiotics are live microorganisms, found in fermented foods and some supplements, that are intended to benefit your health. Prebiotics are the parts of food your body cannot digest, mostly certain fibers, that feed the helpful microorganisms already living in your gut. One is the living guest. The other is the meal that keeps the residents fed.

What are probiotics?

The National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health, part of the NIH, defines probiotics as live microorganisms intended to have health benefits when consumed or applied to the body. They are found in yogurt and other fermented foods, and in dietary supplements.

Many of these organisms are the same as, or similar to, the ones that naturally live in us. That surprises people. We tend to think of bacteria as germs, yet many of them help digest food and produce vitamins.

One honest detail from the NIH matters here: different types of probiotics may have different effects. What one kind does, another kind may not.

What are prebiotics?

Prebiotics are not alive. The NIH describes them as nondigestible food components that selectively support the growth or activity of desirable microorganisms.

Cleveland Clinic puts it simply. Prebiotics are a food source for your gut's microorganisms. They pass through digestion and reach the colon, where your microbes ferment them. Prebiotic foods are usually high in certain fermentable fibers.

If your gut is a garden, which is how I described it in what is the gut microbiome, then probiotics are like adding new plants, and prebiotics are like tending the soil for what is already growing.

Which foods contain prebiotics and probiotics?

Both show up in ordinary food, which is where I always prefer to begin.

Probiotic foods named by the NIH and Cleveland Clinic include:

  • Yogurt
  • Other fermented foods, such as kombucha and tempeh

Prebiotic foods listed by Cleveland Clinic include:

  • Oats, barley, beans and legumes
  • Onions, garlic, leeks and asparagus
  • Green bananas
  • Apples, raspberries, carrots and tomatoes

You may notice that the second list looks a lot like a plain, colorful plate. That is good news. Caring for your gut does not have to start in the supplement aisle.

Do you need both prebiotics and probiotics?

They work as partners, and most people who eat a variety of plants and some fermented foods are already getting some of each. Products that combine the two are called synbiotics.

Whether you need a supplement is a different question, and an individual one. It depends on your health history, your medications and what your body is telling you. That is a conversation for a qualified practitioner and your physician, not a decision to make from a label.

In my gut health support work, I start with safety, rhythm and food before anything else. The body receives nourishment best when it is not bracing.

Are probiotics and prebiotics safe for everyone?

Not automatically. The NIH notes that probiotics have a long history of apparently safe use, particularly in healthy people, and also that the risk of harmful effects is greater in people with severe illnesses or compromised immune systems. It specifically urges caution with premature infants and seriously ill patients.

The NIH also points out that many probiotics are sold as dietary supplements, which do not require FDA approval before they are marketed, and that some products have been found to contain microorganisms not listed on the label. You can read the full NIH fact sheet on probiotics.

Prebiotic foods are gentler ground, though Cleveland Clinic advises adding them gradually, because a sudden increase can bring gas and bloating.

Please talk with your physician before starting a probiotic if you are pregnant, have a weakened immune system, are seriously ill, or are choosing for a baby or young child.

A gentle practice: this week, add one prebiotic food to one meal a day. A few slices of apple. A spoonful of oats. Some onion in the pan. Go slowly and notice how your body responds. Your gut likes to be introduced to change, not surprised by it.

What does the science say about gut supplements?

It says: promising, and unfinished. The NIH is plain that a great deal of research has been done, yet in most instances we still do not know which probiotics are helpful, how much a person would need, or who is most likely to benefit. Cleveland Clinic says something similar about prebiotics, that much is still being discovered.

I find that honesty reassuring. It means you can let go of the pressure to find the perfect product. A steady rhythm of real food, rest and unhurried meals is where I point people first, and I explain why in how to improve gut health naturally.

FAQ

What is the main difference between prebiotics and probiotics?

Probiotics are live microorganisms. Prebiotics are nondigestible parts of food, mostly certain fibers, that feed the helpful microorganisms in your gut.

Can you get prebiotics and probiotics from food?

Yes. Fermented foods such as yogurt contain probiotics, and foods such as oats, beans, onions, garlic and apples contain prebiotic fibers.

What are synbiotics?

The NIH describes synbiotics as products that combine probiotics and prebiotics.
