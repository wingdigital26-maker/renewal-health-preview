Naturopath vs Homeopath: What Is the Difference?

A naturopath supports the body through foundations like nutrition, sleep, movement, and stress care, sometimes alongside herbs. A homeopath uses highly diluted remedies chosen for your individual pattern of symptoms. The two approaches differ in method, but many practitioners, including me, draw on both as support alongside your medical care.

What is the difference between a naturopath and a homeopath?

The simplest way I can say it is that naturopathy and homeopathy are two different languages. Naturopathy speaks to the tangible needs of your biology: what you eat, how you sleep, how you move, and how your body clears what it is finished with. Homeopathy works through a single, carefully chosen remedy matched to your whole pattern.

Here is how they compare in plain terms:

  • Focus: Naturopathy looks at the terrain of your daily life. Homeopathy looks at your individual pattern of symptoms.
  • Tools: Naturopaths often work with food, lifestyle, herbs, and supplements. Homeopaths work with highly diluted remedies.
  • Origins: Naturopathy grew out of traditional practices popular in 19th century Europe. Homeopathy was developed in Germany more than 200 years ago.

Neither one replaces your physician. That role, evaluating and medically managing disease, stays with your doctor.

What does a naturopath do?

A naturopath asks what may be driving a symptom, and looks first at the basics your body was designed to receive. The National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health describes naturopathy as aiming to address root causes, based on the belief that symptoms are the body's way of signaling a problem. It often includes physical activity, a well-balanced diet, and stress management.

I wrote a fuller guide to what a naturopath does if you want to know what working with one tends to look like.

What does a homeopath do?

A homeopath spends a long time listening. Two people with the same complaint may receive two different remedies, because the choice rests on the whole person: how they sleep, what makes things better or worse, how they carry stress.

Homeopathy rests on two ideas. The first is the law of similars, the notion that a substance causing certain symptoms in a healthy person may be used, in tiny amounts, for similar symptoms. The second is the minimum dose. Many remedies are so diluted that no molecules of the original substance remain. I describe homeopathy as informational rather than chemical, and my plain-English primer on homeopathy goes further.

What does the research say about each one?

You deserve a straight answer here, so I will give you one. The National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health states that there is little evidence to support homeopathy as an effective treatment for any specific health condition. It also notes that some products labeled homeopathic can contain substantial amounts of active ingredients, which may cause side effects or drug interactions.

The foundations naturopathy leans on, like sleep, whole foods, and movement, are widely encouraged in medicine. Herbs and supplements are a different matter, since they vary in quality and can interact with prescriptions.

Please do not take any of this as a reason to delay care. Keep your physician in the loop, tell them about anything you are taking, and stay current with the care they recommend. Never stop or change a prescription on your own.

Can you see a naturopath and a homeopath at the same time?

You can, and sometimes one person offers both. If you see two practitioners, make sure each one knows what the other has suggested, and that your physician knows about both.

In my own work, the two sit together. I am a Clinical Homeopath and Naturopath, and I think about them as support for the body and the whole person, offered side by side. Tending the terrain gives the body what it needs to feel safe. You can read how they fit together in my guide to naturopathy and homeopathy for whole-person support.

How do you choose the right practitioner?

Titles and training vary widely in this field, and rules differ by state. NCCIH explains that licensed naturopathic physicians complete graduate-level study at an accredited naturopathic medical school, while traditional naturopaths may train through non-accredited programs of varying length and are not eligible for licensing.

So bring your questions. A good practitioner will welcome them:

  • What is your training, and what are you credentialed to do?
  • How will you work alongside my physician?
  • What will you do if something needs medical attention?
  • Do you make any promises about results? (A careful practitioner will not.)

You are welcome to ask all of this of me. You can read about my story and training before you ever reach out.

FAQ

Is a naturopath a doctor?

Not always. A licensed naturopathic physician completes graduate-level training, while a traditional naturopath does not hold that license. Rules vary by state. Either way, neither one replaces your primary physician, so ask what a practitioner is credentialed to do.

Which is better, a naturopath or a homeopath?

Neither is better for everyone, because they work in different ways. Start with what you are looking for, ask about training, and keep your physician involved. Many people prefer someone who can look at both their daily foundations and their individual pattern.

Is homeopathy safe to use with medication?

Ask your physician or pharmacist first. Highly diluted remedies are different from products that only carry the label, and some products labeled homeopathic contain active ingredients that can interact with medication. Tell your care team about everything you take.
